## The finished pizza

A Neapolitan pizza has a thin, soft centre and a tall, soft, puffy rim. Here is what to expect:

- **Centre:** thin and soft. It bends, and it is not crisp like a cracker.
- **Rim (crust):** tall, airy and tender, with some brown to dark spots from the heat.
- **Toppings:** few and simple, so you can taste the dough.
- **At home:** a real Neapolitan oven runs at {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_oven_c.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_oven_c.range.1 }} degrees and bakes the pizza in {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_bake_s.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_bake_s.range.1 }} seconds. Your oven reaches {{ recipes.oven.max_c.value }} degrees, so the bake takes {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.bake.time_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.bake.time_min.range.1 }} minutes. The result is **Neapolitan-style**, not the original. The rim is a little firmer and the colour is less dark. It is still a very good pizza.

The original recipe is about taste and texture, not about exact numbers. All amounts and times here are a starting point. Bake once, look at the dough and the crust, and adjust next time.

## Choosing a method

In Naples the dough usually ferments {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_ferment_h.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_ferment_h.range.1 }} hours at room temperature, with very little yeast. Fermentation is the yeast eating sugar and making gas and flavour. You can choose from three methods. You choose the method on the Home page, and the Ingredients card on this page shows the amounts for it. To try another method, press "Change" at the top of the page.

**Room-temperature method, {{ recipes.methods.room_8_12h.total_ferment_h.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.methods.room_8_12h.total_ferment_h.range.1 }} hours: closest to Naples.** The dough rises at room temperature (about {{ recipes.oven.room_c.value }} degrees), first as one piece and then as balls.

- **Pros:** the way it is made in Naples. Deep flavour from the slow rise.
- **Cons:** you must plan the day, and the timing depends on your room. A warmer room means a faster rise. The yeast amount for this method is the least certain one in this guide.

**Fridge method, {{ recipes.methods.cold_24h.fridge_h.value }} hours: the easiest to plan.** The dough rests in the fridge (about {{ recipes.oven.fridge_c.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.oven.fridge_c.range.1 }} degrees), where the yeast works slowly.

- **Pros:** deep flavour, and the fridge keeps the dough safe if your plans change. On baking day there is little to do.
- **Cons:** you need fridge space, and the yeast amount has to be exact. That is why this method uses a yeast solution.

**Warm 2-hour method: when you are in a hurry.** You mix the dough and bake about two hours later.

- **Pros:** ready the same day.
- **Cons:** the taste is simpler and more bread-like. It is the furthest from the original.
- **Needs at least {{ recipes.methods.warm_2h.min_pizzas }} pizzas.** For one pizza the yeast would weigh less than a kitchen scale can show.

**In short:** if you have time, choose the room-temperature method for the closest result, or the fridge method for the easiest plan. If you want to eat today, the warm method still makes a tasty pizza.

## Flour and yeast

**Flour.** Use the same Pizzamehl (pizza flour, often marked Tipo 00) as for the New York pizza. The page of the New York pizza explains how to read the protein on the pack, in the section "Flour and yeast".

**Yeast.** The Ingredients card shows the yeast you chose on the Home page (dry or fresh). The page of the New York pizza describes both kinds in its section "Flour and yeast". The room-temperature and fridge methods use a yeast solution, because the yeast is too little for a 1 g scale. The first step of those methods explains it.

- **Sugar:** the original dough has no sugar. This home recipe adds a little ({{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.dough.sugar.value }}% of the flour weight, the Ingredients card shows the grams), because a {{ recipes.oven.max_c.value }} degree oven browns the pizza less than a real Neapolitan oven. You can leave it out.
- **Oil:** none. This dough has no oil.

> Warning: The yeast amounts are a starting point, not a fixed rule. Sources differ by up to {{ recipes.yeast.source_spread_x.value }} times for the same schedule. The right amount depends on your room or fridge temperature and the brand of yeast. Bake once, look at the dough and the crust, and adjust the amount next time.

## Room-temperature method {methods=room_8_12h}

You mix the dough in the morning and bake in the evening, or the other way round. The dough rises at room temperature for {{ recipes.methods.room_8_12h.total_ferment_h.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.methods.room_8_12h.total_ferment_h.range.1 }} hours in total. Take the amounts from the Ingredients card on this page.

> Warning: Room temperature changes the speed a lot. This plan is for a room of about {{ recipes.oven.room_c.value }} degrees. If the room is warmer than {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.hot_room_c.value }} degrees, make both rises shorter and watch the dough.

1. **Make the yeast solution, then mix the cool water, the yeast solution and the flour.** Pour the cool water (about {{ recipes.methods.room_8_12h.water_temp_c.value }} degrees) into a bowl. Add the amount of yeast solution from the Ingredients card. Add the flour and stir only until you see no dry flour.

    > Why: The yeast amount is so small that a kitchen scale cannot weigh it. So you dissolve a whole {{ recipes.yeast.solution.dry.yeast_g.value }} g packet of dry yeast in {{ recipes.yeast.solution.dry.water_g.value }} g of lukewarm water. With fresh yeast, use half of a {{ recipes.yeast.solution.fresh.cube_g.value }} g cube ({{ recipes.yeast.solution.fresh.yeast_g.value }} g) in {{ recipes.yeast.solution.fresh.water_g.value }} g of water. Then you weigh out a part of the solution. The solution counts as part of the water, so the Ingredients card already takes it off the water.
2. **Rest for {{ recipes.methods.room_8_12h.autolyse_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.methods.room_8_12h.autolyse_min.range.1 }} minutes, covered.** This rest is called autolyse.

    > Why: Water and the enzymes in the flour start building gluten by themselves, so you need less kneading.
3. **Add the salt and the sugar, then knead for {{ recipes.methods.room_8_12h.knead_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.methods.room_8_12h.knead_min.range.1 }} minutes.** Work them into the dough until it is even. Kneading is explained in the section "Kneading" of the New York pizza page. Do not add oil.

    > Why: Salt strengthens the gluten and keeps the yeast in check. Gluten is the stretchy net that holds the gas of the yeast.
4. **First rise, as one piece: {{ recipes.methods.room_8_12h.bulk_min.value }} minutes.** Put the dough in a container with a lid (see "Container, oil and cover" on the New York pizza page) and leave it at room temperature.

    > Why: Rising means the dough gets bigger and puffier from the gas the yeast makes. A slow rise at room temperature builds flavour.
5. **Divide the dough into balls of about {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.ball_g.value }} g.** Pull the edges to the middle, turn the dough over and roll it with your palm on the table until the surface is tight and smooth. Put each ball in its own container with a lid.

    > Why: A tight surface keeps the gas in, and the ball opens more easily later.
6. **Let the balls rest, covered, for {{ recipes.methods.room_8_12h.final_rest_min.value }} minutes.** The whole rise, from mixing to baking, takes {{ recipes.methods.room_8_12h.total_ferment_h.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.methods.room_8_12h.total_ferment_h.range.1 }} hours.

    > Why: In the balls the dough keeps rising slowly and the gluten relaxes. A relaxed ball opens easily and does not shrink back.
7. **Start heating the oven and the baking surface {{ recipes.oven.preheat_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.oven.preheat_min.range.1 }} minutes before baking.** Put a pizza stone, a baking steel or a cast-iron pan on the upper rack (about the upper third of the oven). Set the oven to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.bake.temp_c.value }} degrees, with top and bottom heat (in German ovens: Ober-/Unterhitze) and **no fan** (Umluft).

    > Why: A surface that is not fully hot gives a pale, soft base. The fan dries the dough and burns the top before the base is ready.
8. **Open the dough with your fingertips.** Open it only when the oven is hot. Sprinkle about {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.bench_flour_g.value }} g of flour on the table, put the ball on it and press from the middle outwards. **Leave a rim of {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_rim_cm.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_rim_cm.range.1 }} cm untouched.** Stretch and turn the dough by hand until it is {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.shape_diameter_cm.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.shape_diameter_cm.range.1 }} cm wide. If it springs back, rest it for {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.shrink_rest_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.shrink_rest_min.range.1 }} minutes.

    > Why: The untouched rim keeps its air and puffs in the oven. A tall, soft rim is the mark of a Neapolitan pizza.

    > Warning: Do not use a rolling pin. It presses the air out of the dough, and the rim does not puff.
9. **Put on the sauce and the cheese.** Spread {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.toppings.0.amount.value }} g of sauce thinly from the middle outwards and scatter {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.toppings.1.amount.value }} g of cheese on it (see Toppings). Work fast.

    > Why: Dough that waits under wet toppings gets soggy and sticks to the board.
10. **Slide the pizza onto the hot surface fast.** Move it from a board or peel (the wooden or metal paddle for pizzas) onto the surface.

    > Why: Every time the oven door opens, heat is lost. Be quick.

    > Safety: Check the highest temperature printed on your baking paper (Backpapier). Common brands are rated to {{ recipes.safety.parchment_max_c.value }} degrees, which is below this oven. This recipe uses the grill at the end, and baking paper must never go under the grill or touch the heating elements. So bake without paper: open the dough on a board dusted with semolina or flour and slide it onto the hot surface.

    > Safety: Extra-virgin olive oil starts to smoke at about {{ recipes.safety.evoo_smoke_c.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.safety.evoo_smoke_c.range.1 }} degrees, and this oven is at {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.bake.temp_c.value }} degrees. Do not oil the hot surface or pan. This dough has no oil, so there is nothing to worry about inside the dough. If you want oil on the pizza, add a little after baking.
11. **Bake for {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.bake.time_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.bake.time_min.range.1 }} minutes, and use the grill at the end.** Bake with top and bottom heat. For the last {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.bake.grill.last_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.bake.grill.last_min.range.1 }} minutes switch on the grill so the top and the rim get colour. Stay at the oven and watch. The pizza must spend no more than {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.bake.grill.max_min.value }} minutes in total under the grill.

    > Why: A {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.bake.temp_c.value }} degree oven is short on heat from above. A real Neapolitan oven bakes at a much higher temperature, so the grill replaces some of that heat.

    > Tip: The pizza is done when the rim has risen and has brown to dark spots, and the base is firm. Take it out before the spots turn black.
12. **Second pizza:** keep its ball covered until the first pizza is out and the surface is hot again, which takes about {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.second_pizza_wait_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.second_pizza_wait_min.range.1 }} minutes. Only then open it and put on the toppings.

    > Why: Dough that waits open on the board gets wet and sticks.
13. **After baking add the basil.** Put on {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.toppings.2.amount.value }} g of fresh basil.

    > Why: Basil burns and turns bitter in the oven.

## Toppings

Neapolitan toppings are simple. Make the sauce from crushed tomatoes from a tin and a pinch of salt. Use it **raw**, because it cooks in the oven. The Ingredients card shows the dough for the size and number of pizzas you chose on the Home page. The amounts below are for one pizza.

- **Sauce:** {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.toppings.0.amount.value }} g
- **Mozzarella** (dried, grated): {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.toppings.1.amount.value }} g, or {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.toppings.1.amount.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.toppings.1.amount.range.1 }} g
- **Fresh basil**, after baking: {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.toppings.2.amount.value }} g

> Tip: If your mozzarella is in water, tear it into pieces {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.cheese_dry_lead_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.cheese_dry_lead_min.range.1 }} minutes before and dry it with kitchen paper. Otherwise the pizza turns watery.

Order:

1. Spread the sauce thinly with the back of a spoon, from the middle outwards. Leave {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_rim_cm.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_rim_cm.range.1 }} cm of bare dough around the edge.
   > Why: The bare rim puffs up and stays soft and airy.
2. Scatter the cheese evenly over the sauce.
3. Bake (see the step list at the top of this page).
4. After baking add the basil.
   > Why: Basil burns and turns bitter in the oven.

More toppings make a worse Neapolitan pizza. In a {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.bake.temp_c.value }} degree oven the pizza has only a few minutes, and wet or heavy toppings keep the middle wet and the rim flat.

## Troubleshooting

- **The rim did not puff.** You pressed the rim when you shaped it, or the dough had not risen enough. Open the dough from the middle and leave the rim alone.
- **The base is pale or stays soft.** The surface was not hot enough, or there is too much sauce. Heat the surface for {{ recipes.oven.preheat_min.range.1 }} minutes and use no more than {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.toppings.0.amount.value }} g of sauce.
- **The top is pale but the base is browned.** Move the rack higher and use the grill for the last {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.bake.grill.last_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.bake.grill.last_min.range.1 }} minutes.
- **The rim or the top burns under the grill.** Use the grill for a shorter time. Never more than {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.bake.grill.max_min.value }} minutes in total.
- **The pizza is not crisp, and the centre is wet.** There is too much sauce or cheese, or the toppings are wet. Use less, and dry the mozzarella.
- **The dough springs back.** The gluten is tight, or the dough is cold. Rest it for {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.shrink_rest_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.shrink_rest_min.range.1 }} minutes.
- **The dough tears.** It is cold or the gluten is weak. Rest it, or patch the hole with a small piece of dough.
- **A sharp smell of alcohol or sourness.** The dough rose too much. Next time use less yeast or less time. In a warm room shorten the rise.
- **The dough is very sticky or very stiff.** See the sections "The dough is sticky" and "The dough is dry" under "Kneading" on the New York pizza page.
- **The cheese gives off water.** The cheese was wet. Use dried cheese or dry it with kitchen paper.

## What the original rules say

The real Neapolitan pizza has rules from the AVPN (Associazione Verace Pizza Napoletana, an association in Naples that protects the original pizza). These are the facts this guide uses. They are for orientation. Your home pizza does not have to follow them.

- **Fermentation:** {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_ferment_h.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_ferment_h.range.1 }} hours in total. The rules do not name a temperature, and in Naples it is room temperature.
- **Ball and size:** a ball of {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.ball_g.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.ball_g.range.1 }} g, and a pizza of {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_diameter_cm.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_diameter_cm.range.1 }} cm. This guide uses {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.ball_g.value }} g and {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.shape_diameter_cm.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.shape_diameter_cm.range.1 }} cm.
- **Rim:** {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_rim_cm.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_rim_cm.range.1 }} cm.
- **Oven:** {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_oven_c.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_oven_c.range.1 }} degrees, and a bake of {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_bake_s.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_bake_s.range.1 }} seconds. A home oven cannot do this.
- **Ingredients:** flour, water, salt and yeast. No oil and no sugar. This is the widely repeated rule from secondary sources. The AVPN page that was checked for this guide does not list the forbidden ingredients. This home recipe adds a little sugar for colour.
- **Water:** the rules give about {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.dough.hydration.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.dough.hydration.range.1 }}% water to flour. This guide uses {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.dough.hydration.value }}%. Many home recipes use {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.home_hydration_pct.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.home_hydration_pct.range.1 }}% because a home bake is longer.
