## The finished pizza

A Neapolitan pizza has a thin, soft centre and a tall, soft, puffy rim. All temperatures on this site are in degrees Celsius. Here is what to expect:

- **Centre:** thin and soft. It bends. It is not crisp like a cracker.
- **Rim (the edge of the crust):** tall, airy and tender, with some brown to dark spots from the heat.
- **At home:** a real Neapolitan oven runs at {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_oven_c.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_oven_c.range.1 }} degrees and bakes the pizza in {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_bake_s.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_bake_s.range.1 }} seconds. A home oven reaches about {{ recipes.oven.max_c.value }} degrees, so the bake takes {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.bake.time_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.bake.time_min.range.1 }} minutes. The result is **Neapolitan-style**, not the original. The rim is a little firmer and less dark. It is still a very good pizza.

The original recipe is about taste and texture, not exact numbers. All amounts and times here are a starting point. Bake once, look at the dough and the crust, and change what you need next time.

## Choosing a fermentation

Fermentation is the yeast eating sugar and making gas and flavour. In Naples the dough usually ferments for {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_ferment_h.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_ferment_h.range.1 }} hours at room temperature, with very little yeast. This page offers three kinds of fermentation. You choose one on the Home page, and the Ingredients card on this page shows the amounts for it. To try another, press "Change" at the top of the page.

**Room fermentation, {{ recipes.methods.room_8_12h.total_ferment_h.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.methods.room_8_12h.total_ferment_h.range.1 }} hours: closest to Naples.** The dough rises at room temperature (about {{ recipes.oven.room_c.value }} degrees), first as one piece and then as balls.

- **Pros:** this is how it is made in Naples. The slow rise gives deep flavour.
- **Cons:** you must plan the day, and the timing depends on your room. A warmer room means a faster rise. The yeast amount for this fermentation is the least certain in this guide.

**Cold fermentation, {{ recipes.methods.cold_24h.fridge_h.value }} hours: the easiest to plan.** The dough rests in the fridge (about {{ recipes.oven.fridge_c.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.oven.fridge_c.range.1 }} degrees), where the yeast works slowly.

- **Pros:** deep flavour. If your plans change, the dough keeps in the fridge about a day longer. On baking day there is little to do.
- **Cons:** you need fridge space, and the yeast amount has to be exact. That is why this fermentation usually uses a yeast solution (the table on the Ingredients card shows a Yeast solution row when you need one).

**Warm fermentation, 2 hours: when you are in a hurry.** You mix the dough and bake about two hours later.

- **Pros:** ready the same day.
- **Cons:** the taste is simpler, more like plain bread. It is the furthest from the original.
- **Needs at least {{ recipes.methods.warm_2h.min_pizzas }} pizzas.** For one pizza the dry yeast is too little to weigh well on a kitchen scale that reads whole grams. This guide keeps the same rule for fresh yeast.

**In short:** if you have time, choose room fermentation for the closest result, or cold fermentation for the easiest plan. If you want to eat today, warm fermentation still makes a tasty pizza.

## Flour and yeast

**Flour.** Use the same pizza flour as for the New York pizza (the pack often says Tipo 00). The section "Flour and yeast" on the [New York recipe page](/en/recipe/new_york/) explains how to read the protein on the pack.

**Yeast.** The Ingredients card shows the yeast you chose on the Home page (dry or fresh). The section "Flour and yeast" on the [New York recipe page](/en/recipe/new_york/) describes both kinds. For a small batch, room and cold fermentation use a yeast solution, because the yeast is too light for a kitchen scale. The step Prepare the yeast shows how to make one, and the table on the Ingredients card shows whether you need it.

- **Sugar:** the original dough has no sugar. This home recipe adds a little ({{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.dough.sugar.value }}% of the flour weight; the Ingredients card shows the grams), because a {{ recipes.oven.max_c.value }} degree oven browns the pizza less than a real Neapolitan oven. You can leave it out.
- **Oil:** none. This dough has no oil inside.

> Warning: The yeast amounts are a starting point, not a fixed rule. Sources differ by up to {{ recipes.yeast.source_spread_x.value }} times for the same schedule. The right amount depends on your room or fridge temperature and on your yeast. Bake once, look at the dough and the crust, and change the amount next time.

## Room fermentation {methods=room_8_12h}

You mix the dough in the morning and bake in the evening, or the other way round. The dough rises at room temperature for {{ recipes.methods.room_8_12h.total_ferment_h.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.methods.room_8_12h.total_ferment_h.range.1 }} hours in total. Take the amounts from the table on the Ingredients card.

> Warning: Room temperature changes the speed a lot. This plan is for a room of about {{ recipes.oven.room_c.value }} degrees. If your room is warmer than {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.hot_room_c.value }} degrees, make both rises shorter and watch the dough.

**You need:**

- A kitchen scale
- A large bowl to mix the dough in
- A jug or small bowl for the yeast solution, only if your table has a Yeast solution row
- A container with a lid for the rise, big enough for the dough to double
- Small containers with lids, one for each ball
- A baking surface: a pizza stone, a baking steel or a cast-iron pan
- A board or pizza peel, with semolina or flour, or baking paper
- Oven gloves

1. **Prepare the yeast**
   1. Put the large bowl on the scale and set the scale to zero. Pour in the water from the Water row. Use cool water, about {{ recipes.methods.room_8_12h.water_temp_c.value }} degrees.
   2. Add the yeast to the same bowl. Your table has one of these two rows:
      - **Yeast solution:** in the jug, stir one {{ recipes.yeast.solution.dry.yeast_g.value }} g packet of dry yeast (or half a {{ recipes.yeast.solution.fresh.cube_g.value }} g cube of fresh yeast) into {{ recipes.yeast.solution.dry.water_g.value }} g of lukewarm water. Wait {{ recipes.yeast.solution.dissolve_wait_min.value }} minutes and stir again. Set the scale to zero again, with the bowl still on it, and pour in the amount from the Yeast solution row. Throw the rest of the solution away.
      - **Dry yeast or Fresh yeast:** weigh that amount and stir it into the water. Crumble fresh yeast first.
   > Why: A small amount of yeast is too light for a kitchen scale, so you weigh part of a yeast solution instead. The Water row already has the solution's water taken off. The salt goes in later, because salt that touches the yeast directly slows it down.

   > Tip: For the yeast solution, lukewarm means {{ recipes.yeast.solution.water_temp_c.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.yeast.solution.water_temp_c.range.1 }} degrees. Stir until no lumps are left, and stir again just before you pour, because the yeast sinks. One gram of water or solution is about one millilitre, so a measuring jug works too (the About column shows ml).

   > Tip: Not sure your yeast is alive? Stir a pinch of it and a pinch of sugar into a little lukewarm water. If foam forms on top within {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.yeast_check_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.yeast_check_min.range.1 }} minutes, the yeast is alive. Instant dry yeast that is within its date needs no test.
2. **Mix the dough**
   1. Add about half of the flour and stir until the mixture is smooth.
   2. Add the rest of the flour in portions, and stir after each one.
   3. Stir only until you see no dry flour.
   > Why: Flour added in portions gets wet evenly, so you get no dry lumps, and the yeast spreads through the dough.
3. **Rest the dough**
   Cover the bowl and leave the dough for {{ recipes.methods.room_8_12h.autolyse_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.methods.room_8_12h.autolyse_min.range.1 }} minutes. This rest is called autolyse.
   > Why: The water and the enzymes in the flour start to build gluten by themselves, so you need less kneading.
4. **Add the salt and knead**
   1. Sprinkle the salt (and the sugar, if you use it) over the dough. Work them in until the dough is even.
   2. Knead for {{ recipes.methods.room_8_12h.knead_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.methods.room_8_12h.knead_min.range.1 }} minutes. The section "Kneading" on the [New York recipe page](/en/recipe/new_york/) shows how. Do not add oil.
   > Why: Salt makes the gluten stronger and slows the yeast down. Gluten is the stretchy net that holds the gas from the yeast.
5. **Let the dough rise**
   Put the dough in a lightly oiled container with a lid (see "Container, oil and cover" on the [New York recipe page](/en/recipe/new_york/)). This oil is only for the container, not for the dough. Leave it as one piece at room temperature for about {{ recipes.methods.room_8_12h.bulk_h.value }} hours, until it is clearly bigger and a little puffy. The time is only a guide: the dough decides, and a warm kitchen is faster. See ["Reading the dough" on the Basics page](/en/basics/#reading-the-dough). Keep the lid on, so the surface does not dry out.
   > Why: The yeast makes gas, and the gas makes the dough bigger and puffier. A slow rise at room temperature builds flavour.

   > Tip: Use a container big enough for the dough to double without touching the lid. Mark the dough level on the outside with tape or a pen, so you can see how much it has grown.
6. **Divide and ball**
   1. Put the whole dough on the scale. The weight should be close to Total dough in the table.
   2. Divide the weight by the number of pizzas. Cut off equal pieces of that weight.
   3. Shape each piece into a ball: pull the edges to the middle, turn it over and roll it under your palm on the table until the surface is tight and smooth. Put each ball in its own lightly oiled container with a lid.
   > Why: Equal balls bake evenly and make pizzas of the same size. A scale is more accurate than your eye. A tight surface keeps the gas in, and the ball opens more easily later.
7. **Rest the balls**
   Leave the balls, covered, for about {{ recipes.methods.room_8_12h.final_rest_h.value }} hours, until they are soft and airy and a floured finger dent fills back slowly. See ["Reading the dough" on the Basics page](/en/basics/#reading-the-dough). The whole rise, from mixing to baking, takes {{ recipes.methods.room_8_12h.total_ferment_h.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.methods.room_8_12h.total_ferment_h.range.1 }} hours.
   > Why: In the balls the dough keeps rising slowly and the gluten relaxes. A relaxed ball opens easily and does not shrink back.
8. **Heat the oven and the baking surface**
   Do this {{ recipes.oven.preheat_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.oven.preheat_min.range.1 }} minutes before baking, while the balls are still resting. Put the baking surface (a pizza stone, a baking steel or a cast-iron pan) on the upper rack, in about the upper third of the oven. Set the oven to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.bake.temp_c.value }} degrees, with top and bottom heat and **no fan**.
   > Why: A surface that is not fully hot gives a pale, soft base. Top and bottom heat sends heat straight into the baking surface from below. In fan mode the bottom element is often off, and the moving air dries and browns the top faster than the base. If your oven has a pizza mode (fan with bottom heat), you can try it and watch the top.
9. **Open the dough**
   Start about {{ recipes.timeline.shape_lead_min.value }} minutes before baking. By then the oven is hot, and the baking surface has almost had its full {{ recipes.oven.preheat_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.oven.preheat_min.range.1 }} minutes. Keep the other balls covered.
   1. Sprinkle about {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.bench_flour_g.value }} g of flour (or semolina: its coarse grains help the dough slide and do not turn pasty; see the Glossary) on the table. Put the ball on it and press from the middle outwards with your fingertips. **Leave a rim of {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_rim_cm.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_rim_cm.range.1 }} cm untouched.**
   2. Stretch the dough with both hands, turning it as you go, until it is the size you chose on the Home page (shown at the top of this page).
   3. If it springs back, let it rest for {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.shrink_rest_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.shrink_rest_min.range.1 }} minutes, then stretch it again.
   > Why: The untouched rim keeps its air, so it puffs in the oven. A tall, soft rim is the sign of a Neapolitan pizza.

   > Warning: Do not use a rolling pin. It presses the air out of the dough, and the rim does not puff.
10. **Dress the pizza**
    Keep it simple.
    1. Move the opened dough onto a board or peel (a flat paddle for pizza) dusted with a little semolina or flour, or onto baking paper (see the Safety notes in the step Slide the pizza in).
    2. Spread a thin layer of sauce and leave the rim bare (about {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_rim_cm.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_rim_cm.range.1 }} cm).
    3. Add cheese and any toppings you like. Do not overload the pizza.
    4. Brush the rim lightly with olive oil. Work fast, because dough that waits on the board or paper sticks.
    > Why: Light toppings let the steam escape, so the centre turns firm instead of wet. Too much topping also makes the pizza heavy and hard to slide. Pat wet toppings dry first, and use dry grated cheese.
11. **Slide the pizza in**
    Slide the pizza from the board or peel onto the baking surface in one quick move, and close the door.
    > Why: Every time you open the oven door, heat is lost. Be quick.

    > Safety: Baking paper is your choice. Without paper, put the opened dough on a board or peel dusted with a little semolina or flour, then slide it onto the hot surface. With paper, check the maximum temperature printed on the pack. Many papers are rated to {{ recipes.safety.parchment_max_c.value }} degrees, which is lower than this oven. Keep the paper away from the heating elements and the oven walls, and trim the edges. Do not use wax paper. If your paper is rated below the oven temperature, pull it out from under the pizza after the first {{ recipes.safety.parchment_pull_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.safety.parchment_pull_min.range.1 }} minutes, when the base has set. If you use paper, skip the grill unless the pack says it is safe.

    > Safety: Extra-virgin olive oil starts to smoke at about {{ recipes.safety.evoo_smoke_c.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.safety.evoo_smoke_c.range.1 }} degrees, and this oven is at {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.bake.temp_c.value }} degrees. Do not oil the hot baking surface. This dough has no oil inside. For the rim, use only a thin film, and choose refined or light olive oil because it smokes less.

    > Safety: Use oven gloves. Pull the rack out only a little to slide the pizza on, and never touch the baking surface; it stays hot for a long time after the oven is off.
12. **Bake**
    Bake for {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.bake.time_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.bake.time_min.range.1 }} minutes with top and bottom heat. For the last {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.bake.grill.last_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.bake.grill.last_min.range.1 }} of these minutes, switch on the grill so the top and the rim get colour. Stay at the oven and watch. Never leave the pizza under the grill for more than {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.bake.grill.max_min.value }} minutes in total. If you use baking paper, skip the grill unless the pack says it is safe.
    > Why: A {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.bake.temp_c.value }} degree oven gives too little heat from above. A real Neapolitan oven is much hotter, so the grill replaces some of that heat.

    > Tip: The pizza is done when the rim has risen and has brown to dark spots, and the base is firm. Take it out before the spots turn black.
13. **Take the pizza out**
    Take it out with the peel or with oven gloves. Add fresh basil and a little olive oil if you like.
14. **Wait for the second pizza**
    Switch the oven back to top and bottom heat. Wait {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.second_pizza_wait_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.second_pizza_wait_min.range.1 }} minutes, so the baking surface gets hot again. Keep the next ball covered until then, and only then open it.
    > Why: Dough that waits on the board or paper gets wet and sticks.

   1. Put the dough in a lightly oiled container with a lid (see "Container, oil and cover" on the [New York recipe page](/en/recipe/new_york/)). This oil is only for the container, not for the dough.

For the last {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.bake.grill.last_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.bake.grill.last_min.range.1 }} of these minutes, switch on the grill so the top and the rim get colour. Stay at the oven and watch. Never leave the pizza under the grill for more than {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.bake.grill.max_min.value }} minutes in total. If you use baking paper, skip the grill unless the pack says it is safe.
    > Why: A {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.bake.temp_c.value }} degree oven gives too little heat from above. A real Neapolitan oven is much hotter, so the grill replaces some of that heat.

    > Tip: The pizza is done when the rim has risen and has brown to dark spots, and the base is firm. Take it out before the spots turn black.
14. **Take the pizza out**
    Take it out with the peel or with oven gloves. Add fresh basil and a little olive oil if you like.
15. **Wait for the second pizza**
    Switch the oven back to top and bottom heat. Wait {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.second_pizza_wait_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.second_pizza_wait_min.range.1 }} minutes, so the baking surface gets hot again. Keep the next ball covered until then, and only then open it.
    > Why: Dough that waits on the board or paper gets wet and sticks.

## Warm fermentation, 2 hours {methods=warm_2h}

You mix the dough and bake about two hours later. Take the amounts from the table on the Ingredients card.

> Warning: This fermentation needs at least {{ recipes.methods.warm_2h.min_pizzas }} pizzas. For one pizza the dry yeast is too little to weigh well on a kitchen scale that reads whole grams. This guide keeps the same rule for fresh yeast. For one pizza, choose another fermentation.

**You need:**

- A kitchen scale
- A large bowl to mix the dough in
- A container with a lid for the rise, big enough for the dough to double
- A baking surface: a pizza stone, a baking steel or a cast-iron pan
- A board or pizza peel, with semolina or flour, or baking paper
- Oven gloves

1. **Prepare the yeast**
   1. Put the large bowl on the scale and set the scale to zero. Pour in the water from the Water row. Use lukewarm water, about {{ recipes.methods.warm_2h.water_temp_c.value }} degrees.
   2. Weigh the yeast from the Dry yeast or Fresh yeast row and stir it into the water. Crumble fresh yeast first.
   > Why: Yeast works fast between {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.yeast_works_c.range.0 }} and {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.yeast_works_c.range.1 }} degrees and starts to die from about {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.yeast_dies_c.value }} degrees, so never use hot water. Lukewarm water feels only slightly warm on your wrist, cooler than a bath. The salt goes in later, because salt that touches the yeast directly slows it down.

   > Tip: Not sure your yeast is alive? Stir a pinch of it and a pinch of sugar into a little lukewarm water. If foam forms on top within {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.yeast_check_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.yeast_check_min.range.1 }} minutes, the yeast is alive. Instant dry yeast that is within its date needs no test.
2. **Mix the dough**
   1. Add about half of the flour and stir until the mixture is smooth.
   2. Add the rest of the flour in portions, and stir after each one.
   3. Stir only until you see no dry flour.
   > Why: Flour added in portions gets wet evenly, so you get no dry lumps, and the yeast spreads through the dough.
3. **Add the salt and knead**
   1. Sprinkle the salt (and the sugar, if you use it) over the dough. Work them in until the dough is even.
   2. Knead for {{ recipes.methods.warm_2h.knead_min.value }} minutes. The section "Kneading" on the [New York recipe page](/en/recipe/new_york/) shows how. Do not add oil.
   > Why: There is little time, so kneading has to build the gluten that a long rest would build. Salt makes the gluten stronger and slows the yeast down.
4. **Let the dough rise**
   1. Put the dough in a lightly oiled container with a lid (see "Container, oil and cover" on the [New York recipe page](/en/recipe/new_york/)). This oil is only for the container, not for the dough.
   2. Leave it in a warm place, at {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.warm_place_c.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.warm_place_c.range.1 }} degrees, for about {{ recipes.methods.warm_2h.bulk_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.methods.warm_2h.bulk_min.range.1 }} minutes. A switched-off oven with the light on works well.
   3. The dough should grow to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.rise_factor.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.rise_factor.range.1 }} times its size. Without a warm place, the rise can take up to {{ recipes.methods.warm_2h.bulk_slow_max_min.value }} minutes: the time is only a guide, so wait for the size, not the clock. See ["Reading the dough" on the Basics page](/en/basics/#reading-the-dough).
   > Why: The yeast makes gas, and the gas makes the dough bigger. This air makes the rim puff in the oven.

   > Tip: Use a container big enough for the dough to double without touching the lid. Mark the dough level on the outside with tape or a pen, so you can see how much it has grown.
5. **Heat the oven and the baking surface**
   Do this about halfway through the rise, so the surface is hot when the balls are ready.
   1. If the dough rises in the oven, take it out first. A plastic container or lid melts in a heating oven.
   2. Put the baking surface (a pizza stone, a baking steel or a cast-iron pan) on the upper rack, in about the upper third of the oven.
   3. Set the oven to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.bake.temp_c.value }} degrees, with top and bottom heat and **no fan**.
   > Why: The baking surface needs {{ recipes.oven.preheat_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.oven.preheat_min.range.1 }} minutes to get fully hot. A surface that is not fully hot gives a pale, soft base. Top and bottom heat sends heat straight into the baking surface from below. In fan mode the bottom element is often off, and the moving air dries and browns the top faster than the base. If your oven has a pizza mode (fan with bottom heat), you can try it and watch the top.
6. **Divide and ball**
   1. Put the whole dough on the scale. The weight should be close to Total dough in the table.
   2. Divide the weight by the number of pizzas. Cut off equal pieces of that weight.
   3. Shape each piece into a ball: pull the edges to the middle, turn it over and roll it under your palm on the table until the surface is tight and smooth.
   > Why: Equal balls bake evenly and make pizzas of the same size. A scale is more accurate than your eye. A tight surface keeps the gas in, and the ball opens more easily later.
7. **Rest the balls**
   Cover the balls (a container with a lid or an upturned bowl works) and leave them for about {{ recipes.methods.warm_2h.final_rest_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.methods.warm_2h.final_rest_min.range.1 }} minutes, until they are soft and relaxed. The dough decides, not the clock. See ["Reading the dough" on the Basics page](/en/basics/#reading-the-dough). The oven keeps heating.
   > Why: The gluten relaxes. A relaxed ball opens easily and does not shrink back.
8. **Open the dough**
   Start about {{ recipes.timeline.shape_lead_min.value }} minutes before baking. By then the oven is hot, and the baking surface has almost had its full {{ recipes.oven.preheat_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.oven.preheat_min.range.1 }} minutes. Keep the other balls covered.
   1. Sprinkle about {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.bench_flour_g.value }} g of flour (or semolina: its coarse grains help the dough slide and do not turn pasty; see the Glossary) on the table. Put the ball on it and press from the middle outwards with your fingertips. **Leave a rim of {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_rim_cm.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_rim_cm.range.1 }} cm untouched.**
   2. Stretch the dough with both hands, turning it as you go, until it is the size you chose on the Home page (shown at the top of this page).
   3. If it springs back, let it rest for {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.shrink_rest_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.shrink_rest_min.range.1 }} minutes, then stretch it again.
   > Why: The untouched rim keeps its air, so it puffs in the oven. A tall, soft rim is the sign of a Neapolitan pizza.

   > Warning: Do not use a rolling pin. It presses the air out of the dough, and the rim does not puff.
9. **Dress the pizza**
   Keep it simple.
   1. Move the opened dough onto a board or peel (a flat paddle for pizza) dusted with a little semolina or flour, or onto baking paper (see the Safety notes in the step Slide the pizza in).
   2. Spread a thin layer of sauce and leave the rim bare (about {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_rim_cm.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_rim_cm.range.1 }} cm).
   3. Add cheese and any toppings you like. Do not overload the pizza.
   4. Brush the rim lightly with olive oil. Work fast, because dough that waits on the board or paper sticks.
   > Why: Light toppings let the steam escape, so the centre turns firm instead of wet. Too much topping also makes the pizza heavy and hard to slide. Pat wet toppings dry first, and use dry grated cheese.
10. **Slide the pizza in**
    Slide the pizza from the board or peel onto the baking surface in one quick move, and close the door.
    > Why: Every time you open the oven door, heat is lost. Be quick.

    > Safety: Baking paper is your choice. Without paper, put the opened dough on a board or peel dusted with a little semolina or flour, then slide it onto the hot surface. With paper, check the maximum temperature printed on the pack. Many papers are rated to {{ recipes.safety.parchment_max_c.value }} degrees, which is lower than this oven. Keep the paper away from the heating elements and the oven walls, and trim the edges. Do not use wax paper. If your paper is rated below the oven temperature, pull it out from under the pizza after the first {{ recipes.safety.parchment_pull_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.safety.parchment_pull_min.range.1 }} minutes, when the base has set. If you use paper, skip the grill unless the pack says it is safe.

    > Safety: Extra-virgin olive oil starts to smoke at about {{ recipes.safety.evoo_smoke_c.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.safety.evoo_smoke_c.range.1 }} degrees, and this oven is at {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.bake.temp_c.value }} degrees. Do not oil the hot baking surface. This dough has no oil inside. For the rim, use only a thin film, and choose refined or light olive oil because it smokes less.

    > Safety: Use oven gloves. Pull the rack out only a little to slide the pizza on, and never touch the baking surface; it stays hot for a long time after the oven is off.
11. **Bake**
    Bake for {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.bake.time_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.bake.time_min.range.1 }} minutes with top and bottom heat. For the last {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.bake.grill.last_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.bake.grill.last_min.range.1 }} of these minutes, switch on the grill so the top and the rim get colour. Stay at the oven and watch. Never leave the pizza under the grill for more than {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.bake.grill.max_min.value }} minutes in total. If you use baking paper, skip the grill unless the pack says it is safe.
    > Why: A {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.bake.temp_c.value }} degree oven gives too little heat from above. A real Neapolitan oven is much hotter, so the grill replaces some of that heat.

    > Tip: The pizza is done when the rim has risen and has brown to dark spots, and the base is firm. Take it out before the spots turn black.
12. **Take the pizza out**
    Take it out with the peel or with oven gloves. Add fresh basil and a little olive oil if you like.
13. **Wait for the second pizza**
    Switch the oven back to top and bottom heat. Wait {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.second_pizza_wait_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.second_pizza_wait_min.range.1 }} minutes, so the baking surface gets hot again. Keep the next ball covered until then, and only then open it.
    > Why: Dough that waits on the board or paper gets wet and sticks.

## Troubleshooting

- **The rim did not puff.** You pressed the rim when you opened the dough, or the dough had not risen enough. Press only the middle and leave the rim alone.
- **The base is pale or stays soft.** The baking surface was not hot enough. Heat it for the full {{ recipes.oven.preheat_min.range.1 }} minutes.
- **The top is pale but the base is browned.** Move the rack higher and use the grill for the last {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.bake.grill.last_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.bake.grill.last_min.range.1 }} minutes.
- **The rim or the top burns under the grill.** Use the grill for a shorter time. Never use it for more than {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.bake.grill.max_min.value }} minutes in total.
- **The dough springs back.** The gluten is tight, or the dough is cold. Let it rest for {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.shrink_rest_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.shrink_rest_min.range.1 }} minutes.
- **The dough tears.** It is cold, or the gluten is weak. Let it rest, or patch the hole with a small piece of dough.
- **A sharp smell of alcohol or sourness.** The dough rose too much. Next time use less yeast or a shorter rise, especially in a warm room.
- **The dough is very sticky or very stiff.** See "The dough is sticky" and "The dough is dry" in the section "Kneading" on the [New York recipe page](/en/recipe/new_york/).

## What the original rules say

The real Neapolitan pizza has rules from the AVPN (Associazione Verace Pizza Napoletana, an association in Naples that protects the original pizza). This guide uses the facts below. They are for information only. Your home pizza does not have to follow them.

- **Fermentation:** {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_ferment_h.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_ferment_h.range.1 }} hours in total. The rules do not name a temperature. In Naples it is room temperature.
- **Ball and size:** a ball of {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.ball_g.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.ball_g.range.1 }} g, and a pizza of {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_diameter_cm.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_diameter_cm.range.1 }} cm. This guide lets you choose {{ recipes.sizes.0.diameter }}, {{ recipes.sizes.1.diameter }} or {{ recipes.sizes.2.diameter }} cm. To get your ball weight, divide Total dough on the Ingredients card by the number of pizzas. The smallest size gives a ball a little under the original minimum, and the largest a little over the maximum.
- **Rim:** {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_rim_cm.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_rim_cm.range.1 }} cm.
- **Oven:** {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_oven_c.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_oven_c.range.1 }} degrees, and a bake of {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_bake_s.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.avpn_bake_s.range.1 }} seconds. A home oven cannot get this hot.
- **Ingredients:** flour, water, salt and yeast. The official EU specification for Pizza Napoletana lists only these for the dough; olive oil goes on top. This home recipe adds a little optional sugar for colour.
- **Water:** the rules give about {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.dough.hydration.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.dough.hydration.range.1 }}% water to flour. This guide uses {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.dough.hydration.value }}%. Many home recipes use {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.home_hydration_pct.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.neapolitan.practice.home_hydration_pct.range.1 }}% water, because a home bake is longer.
