## The finished pizza

A New York pizza is thin to medium in the middle, with a crisp base that still bends. You can fold a slice in half. Here is what to expect:

- **Base:** crisp, but flexible. It does not snap like a cracker.
- **Rim (crust):** ordinary in size. It is not flat like a cracker and not puffed up like a Neapolitan rim.
- **Crumb:** chewy.
- **Browning:** the surface turns golden to light brown. Browning is the colour the heat gives the crust. Sugar and protein react in the heat, and this adds a toasted, slightly sweet taste.
- **At home:** a {{ recipes.oven.max_c.value }} degree oven makes the rim a little more bread-like than in a pizzeria. The base still turns crisp on a hot cast-iron tray.

## Choosing a method

There are two ways to make this dough. Both give a good pizza. They differ in time and taste. You choose the method on the Home page, and the Ingredients card on this page shows the amounts for it. To try another method, press "Change" at the top of the page.

**Warm 2-hour method: when you are in a hurry.** You mix the dough and bake about two hours later.

- **How it works:** more yeast, lukewarm water and {{ recipes.methods.warm_2h.knead_min.value }} minutes of kneading. The yeast works fast, so the dough rises in about an hour.
- **Pros:** ready the same day. No planning.
- **Cons:** the taste is simpler and more bread-like. The browning is a little weaker.
- **Needs at least {{ recipes.methods.warm_2h.min_pizzas }} pizzas.** For one pizza the yeast would weigh less than a kitchen scale can show.

**Cold fermentation: when you can wait until tomorrow.** Fermentation is the yeast eating sugar and making gas and flavour. Cold fermentation means the dough does this slowly in the fridge (about {{ recipes.oven.fridge_c.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.oven.fridge_c.range.1 }} degrees). You can choose from {{ recipes.methods.cold_12_16h.fridge_h.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.methods.cold_12_16h.fridge_h.range.1 }} hours (overnight) up to {{ recipes.methods.cold_72h.fridge_h.value }} hours.

- **How it works:** very little yeast, cold water and lighter kneading. In the cold the yeast works slowly, and the enzymes in the flour have time to break down starch and protein.
- **Pros:** deeper flavour, better browning and a lighter, chewier rim. On baking day there is little to do: take it out, shape it, bake it. If your plans change, the dough keeps in the fridge for longer.
- **Cons:** you must plan ahead, the dough needs fridge space, and the yeast amount has to be exact. That is why this method uses a yeast solution.

**In short:** if you have until tomorrow, choose cold fermentation. If you want to eat today, the warm method still makes a tasty pizza.

## Flour and yeast

**Flour.** Use pizza flour or strong bread flour. The pack of pizza flour often says Tipo 00, which means very finely milled wheat flour.

- **Check the protein.** Look for the line **protein** in the nutrition table. The number is per 100 g of flour. A good value is {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.flour_protein_g.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.flour_protein_g.range.1 }} g. Read the exact number on your pack, because flours differ.
- **Why more protein is better:** more protein means stronger gluten. Gluten is the stretchy net that holds the gas of the yeast. The dough stretches better, tears less when you shape it, and handles a long cold rest better.
- **Not too much:** above {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.flour_protein_max_g.value }} g the dough turns stiff and rubbery.

**Yeast.** Use what you have. The Ingredients card shows the yeast you chose on the Home page (dry or fresh).

- **Dry yeast:** a common packet holds {{ recipes.yeast.solution.dry.yeast_g.value }} g (check your pack). It is instant yeast. It needs no proofing (no waiting in water first). You can stir it straight into the flour or the water. Close an opened packet tightly. Check the text on your packet.
- **Fresh yeast:** a small soft cube, commonly {{ recipes.yeast.solution.fresh.cube_g.value }} g (check your pack), sold in the fridge section. It has a short shelf life. Wrap what is left and keep it in the fridge.
- **Equivalent:** fresh yeast is {{ recipes.yeast.fresh_to_dry.value }} times the amount of dry yeast.

> Warning: The yeast amounts are a starting point, not a fixed rule. Sources differ by up to {{ recipes.yeast.source_spread_x.value }} times for the same schedule. The right amount depends on your fridge temperature and your yeast. Bake once, look at the dough and the crust, and adjust the amount next time.

## Warm 2-hour method {methods=warm_2h}

You mix the dough and bake about two hours later. Take the amounts from the Ingredients card on this page.

> Warning: This method needs at least {{ recipes.methods.warm_2h.min_pizzas }} pizzas. For one pizza the yeast would weigh less than {{ recipes.yeast.min_weigh_g.value }} g, and a scale that shows whole grams cannot measure that. For one pizza use a cold method.

1. **Stir the yeast into lukewarm water.** The water should be about {{ recipes.methods.warm_2h.water_temp_c.value }} degrees. Dry yeast needs no waiting.
   > Why: Yeast works fast between {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.yeast_works_c.range.0 }} and {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.yeast_works_c.range.1 }} degrees and dies above {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.yeast_dies_c.value }} degrees. The water should feel like bath water, not hot.
2. **Add the flour and the salt.** Mix until you see no dry flour.
3. **Add the sugar and the olive oil, then knead for {{ recipes.methods.warm_2h.knead_min.value }} minutes.** Kneading is explained in the Kneading section below.
   > Why: There is little time, so kneading has to build the gluten that a long rest would build otherwise.
4. **First rise.** Put the dough in an oiled container with a lid (see the section Container, oil and cover). Leave it in a warm place for {{ recipes.methods.warm_2h.bulk_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.methods.warm_2h.bulk_min.range.1 }} minutes. {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.warm_place_c.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.warm_place_c.range.1 }} degrees is good. The dough should grow to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.rise_factor.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.rise_factor.range.1 }} times its size, with a soft, puffy surface.
   > Why: Rising means the dough gets bigger and puffier from the gas the yeast makes. Rising before shaping gives the dough air, so the rim puffs in the oven.
5. **Divide the dough into two equal pieces and shape each into a ball** (see the section Shaping).
6. **Rest the balls, covered, for {{ recipes.methods.warm_2h.final_rest_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.methods.warm_2h.final_rest_min.range.1 }} minutes.** Start heating the oven and the tray at the same time (see the section Oven and baking surface).
   > Why: The gluten relaxes. A relaxed ball opens easily and does not shrink back when you stretch it.
7. **Shape the dough** (see Shaping).
8. **Dress the pizza.** Spread a thin layer of sauce and leave the rim bare (about {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.rim_cm.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.rim_cm.range.1 }} cm). Add cheese and any toppings you like, then brush the rim lightly with olive oil. Work fast, because dough that waits on the paper sticks.
   > Why: Keep it light. A heavy or wet topping holds steam under the dough, so the base stays soft and the pizza sticks to the paper. Dry wet ingredients or pre-cook them, and use dry grated cheese or dry it first. Use olive oil only as a thin film on the rim; a refined or light olive oil resists smoke better (see the Safety note below).

    > Tip: After adding the cheese, a small pinch of garlic powder sprinkled on top tastes good. It is optional. Garlic powder burns less than fresh garlic.
9. **Bake** (see Oven and baking surface).

    > Safety: Check the highest temperature printed on the pack of your baking paper before you use it. Many baking papers are rated to {{ recipes.safety.parchment_max_c.value }} degrees, which is below this oven. Never put baking paper under the grill, and do not let it touch the heating elements or the oven walls. If your paper is not rated high enough, bake without paper: open the dough on a board dusted with semolina or flour and slide it onto the hot surface.

    > Safety: Extra-virgin olive oil starts to smoke at about {{ recipes.safety.evoo_smoke_c.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.safety.evoo_smoke_c.range.1 }} degrees, and this oven is at {{ recipes.styles.new_york.bake.temp_c.value }} degrees. Do not oil the hot surface or pan. Oil mixed into the dough is fine.
10. **Take the pizza out and let it rest for {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.rest_before_cut_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.rest_before_cut_min.range.1 }} minutes before you cut it.** Leave it uncovered, on a rack if you have one. While it rests, add what you like that is fresh or delicate: a drizzle of olive oil, fresh herbs such as basil, grated hard cheese, chili flakes, or a squeeze of lemon on some toppings.
   > Why: Steam escapes and the cheese sets, so the toppings do not slide off. An uncovered base keeps its crispness. Cut with a sharp pizza wheel or a large knife. Fresh or delicate things go on after the bake so they do not burn or wilt.


> Tip: A switched-off oven with the light on is a good warm place. Make sure the oven is not heating while the dough is inside.

## Cold fermentation {methods=cold_12_16h,cold_24h,cold_48h,cold_72h}

You make the dough one or more days before baking. Take the amounts from the Ingredients card on this page. It shows the amounts for the fermentation time you chose on the Home page. Steps 1 to 6 are the first day. Steps 7 to 12 are baking day.

1. **Make the yeast solution, then mix the cold water, the yeast solution and the flour.** Pour the cold water (about {{ recipes.methods.cold_24h.water_temp_c.value }} degrees) into a bowl. Add the amount of yeast solution from the Ingredients card. Add the flour and stir only until you see no dry flour.
   > Why: The yeast amount is so small that a kitchen scale cannot weigh it. So you dissolve a whole {{ recipes.yeast.solution.dry.yeast_g.value }} g packet of dry yeast in {{ recipes.yeast.solution.dry.water_g.value }} g of lukewarm water. With fresh yeast, use half of a {{ recipes.yeast.solution.fresh.cube_g.value }} g cube ({{ recipes.yeast.solution.fresh.yeast_g.value }} g) in {{ recipes.yeast.solution.fresh.water_g.value }} g of water. Then you weigh out a part of the solution. The solution counts as part of the water, so the Ingredients card already takes it off the cold water.
2. **Rest for {{ recipes.methods.cold_24h.autolyse_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.methods.cold_24h.autolyse_min.range.1 }} minutes, covered.** This rest is called autolyse.
   > Why: Water and the enzymes in the flour start building gluten by themselves, so you need less kneading.
3. **Add the salt, the sugar and the olive oil.** Work them into the dough until it is even. Then knead for {{ recipes.methods.cold_24h.knead_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.methods.cold_24h.knead_min.range.1 }} minutes (see Kneading). If you prefer stretch and fold instead of kneading, skip the kneading and go to step 4.
   > Why: Salt strengthens the gluten and keeps the yeast in check.
4. **Rise at room temperature** (about {{ recipes.oven.room_c.value }} degrees) for {{ recipes.methods.cold_24h.room_rest_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.methods.cold_24h.room_rest_min.range.1 }} minutes, until the dough is about {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.cold_rise_pct.value }}% bigger. If you chose stretch and fold, do it during this rise: {{ recipes.methods.cold_24h.stretch_fold.rounds.value }} rounds, the first one right now, the next ones {{ recipes.methods.cold_24h.stretch_fold.gap_min.value }} minutes apart. After the last round leave the dough alone until the rise is over.
   > Why: The yeast needs a short warm start before it goes into the cold. The folds make the dough strong while it rises, so the rounds and the rise are one period, not two.
5. **Divide the dough into two equal pieces and shape each into a ball** (see Shaping). Put each ball in its own oiled container with a lid (see Container, oil and cover).
6. **Put the containers in the fridge.** Choose how long: {{ recipes.methods.cold_12_16h.fridge_h.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.methods.cold_12_16h.fridge_h.range.1 }} hours (overnight), {{ recipes.methods.cold_24h.fridge_h.value }} hours, {{ recipes.methods.cold_48h.fridge_h.value }} hours or {{ recipes.methods.cold_72h.fridge_h.value }} hours. The yeast amount on the Ingredients card is for the time you chose on the Home page. To change the time, press "Change" at the top of the page. {{ recipes.methods.cold_72h.fridge_h.value }} hours is the longest this guide uses.
   > Why: In the fridge the yeast works slowly, and the enzymes have time to build flavour. The result is a deeper taste and better browning.
7. **On baking day, take the dough out of the fridge {{ recipes.methods.cold_24h.take_out_lead_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.methods.cold_24h.take_out_lead_min.range.1 }} minutes before you shape it.** Keep the lid closed.
   > Why: Cold dough does not stretch and tears. This is the most common mistake in cold fermentation.
8. **Start heating the oven and the tray {{ recipes.oven.preheat_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.oven.preheat_min.range.1 }} minutes before baking** (see Oven and baking surface).
9. **Shape the dough** (see Shaping).
10. **Dress the pizza.** Spread a thin layer of sauce and leave the rim bare (about {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.rim_cm.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.rim_cm.range.1 }} cm). Add cheese and any toppings you like, then brush the rim lightly with olive oil. Work fast, because dough that waits on the paper sticks.
   > Why: Keep it light. A heavy or wet topping holds steam under the dough, so the base stays soft and the pizza sticks to the paper. Dry wet ingredients or pre-cook them, and use dry grated cheese or dry it first. Use olive oil only as a thin film on the rim; a refined or light olive oil resists smoke better (see the Safety note below).

    > Tip: After adding the cheese, a small pinch of garlic powder sprinkled on top tastes good. It is optional. Garlic powder burns less than fresh garlic.
11. **Bake** (see Oven and baking surface).

    > Safety: Check the highest temperature printed on the pack of your baking paper before you use it. Many baking papers are rated to {{ recipes.safety.parchment_max_c.value }} degrees, which is below this oven. Never put baking paper under the grill, and do not let it touch the heating elements or the oven walls. If your paper is not rated high enough, bake without paper: open the dough on a board dusted with semolina or flour and slide it onto the hot surface.

    > Safety: Extra-virgin olive oil starts to smoke at about {{ recipes.safety.evoo_smoke_c.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.safety.evoo_smoke_c.range.1 }} degrees, and this oven is at {{ recipes.styles.new_york.bake.temp_c.value }} degrees. Do not oil the hot surface or pan. Oil mixed into the dough is fine.
12. **Take the pizza out and let it rest for {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.rest_before_cut_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.rest_before_cut_min.range.1 }} minutes before you cut it.** Leave it uncovered, on a rack if you have one. While it rests, add what you like that is fresh or delicate: a drizzle of olive oil, fresh herbs such as basil, grated hard cheese, chili flakes, or a squeeze of lemon on some toppings.
   > Why: Steam escapes and the cheese sets, so the toppings do not slide off. An uncovered base keeps its crispness. Cut with a sharp pizza wheel or a large knife. Fresh or delicate things go on after the bake so they do not burn or wilt.


> Tip: If you put the dough in the fridge as one piece, take it out at the same time, but divide it and shape the balls first. Cold dough shapes more easily. Then let the balls rest, covered.

> Tip: Mix the yeast solution right before you use it, and throw away what is left. No source says how long a yeast solution keeps, so this guide gives no shelf life.

## Kneading

Kneading means pulling, pressing and folding the dough to build gluten, the stretchy net that holds the gas. The goal is to turn a sticky, lumpy mass into a smooth, soft ball.

### By hand ({{ recipes.methods.cold_24h.knead_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.methods.cold_24h.knead_min.range.1 }} minutes)

1. Put the dough on a clean table. **Do not add extra flour to the table.**
   > Why: Extra flour makes the dough stiff.
2. Press the dough away from you with the heel of your hand and stretch it.
3. Take the far edge and fold it over the dough.
4. Turn the dough a quarter turn.
5. Repeat the four moves (press, stretch, fold, turn) one after the other.

### How the dough feels

- **Minutes {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.kneading_early_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.kneading_early_min.range.1 }}:** lumpy and very sticky. It sticks to your hands and the table. This is normal.
- **Minutes {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.kneading_mid_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.kneading_mid_min.range.1 }}:** less sticky. The dough starts to hold together.
- **Minutes {{ recipes.methods.cold_24h.knead_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.methods.cold_24h.knead_min.range.1 }}:** smooth, soft and stretchy. It still feels a little sticky, but a clean, dry hand does not pick up dough. When you press it with a finger, it springs back slowly.

### Windowpane test

Take a small piece of dough and stretch it slowly between your fingers. If it turns thin and see-through without tearing, the gluten is strong enough. If it tears fast, knead for {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.windowpane_extra_knead_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.windowpane_extra_knead_min.range.1 }} more minutes, or rest the dough for {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.windowpane_rest_min.value }} minutes.

### The dough is sticky

The dough has {{ recipes.styles.new_york.dough.hydration.value }}% water, so it is soft and a little sticky. At first you will want to add flour. **Do not.** Extra flour makes the dough stiff and dense. Do this instead:

- Wet your hands a little. Keep a bowl of water next to you.
- Use a plastic scraper to lift the dough from the table.
- Rest the dough for {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.sticky_rest_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.sticky_rest_min.range.1 }} minutes, then knead again. The gluten gets stronger while the dough rests, and the stickiness drops.
- If after {{ recipes.methods.cold_24h.knead_min.range.1 }} minutes of kneading the dough is still as loose as cake batter and sticks to a clean hand: rest it covered, then do {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.sticky_fold_rounds.range.0 }} or {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.sticky_fold_rounds.range.1 }} rounds of stretch and fold, {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.sticky_fold_gap_min.value }} minutes apart. Only if it is still very loose, add {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.sticky_add_flour_g.value }} g of flour and knead well. Never add more than {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.sticky_max_flour_g.value }} g of flour in total.

### The dough is dry

If the dough is stiff, cracks or tires your hands:

- Wet your hand and add {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.dry_add_water_g.value }} g of water. The dough turns slippery first, but the water soaks in as you knead.
- Rest it for {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.dry_rest_min.value }} minutes.
- If it is still stiff, add {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.dry_add_water_g.value }} g of water again. Never add more than {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.dry_max_water_g.value }} g in total.
- Common causes: flour with more protein soaks up more water, the water or flour was not weighed exactly, or the air in the room is dry.

### Stretch and fold

Use this when kneading by hand is hard or the dough is loose. One round looks like this:

6. Wet your hand.
7. Take one side of the dough and pull it up gently, without tearing it.
8. Fold it over the middle of the dough.
9. Turn the bowl a quarter turn and repeat.
10. Do this 4 times. That is one round.

Between the rounds, cover the dough.

### With a mixer

Use low speed for {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.machine_knead_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.machine_knead_min.range.1 }} minutes. The dough should come off the bowl wall and gather around the hook. Do not mix for longer, because the machine warms the dough.

## Container, oil and cover

### Oiled container

An oiled container keeps the dough from sticking, so it comes out without tearing. It also keeps the surface from drying.

1. Take about {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.oil_per_container_g.value }} g of liquid oil (olive oil or another liquid oil) for each container.
2. Wipe a **thin layer** over the bottom and the walls with a finger or kitchen paper. No oil should pool at the bottom.
3. Put the ball in and turn it once, so the top has a little oil too.
4. Give each ball its own container, or leave a gap of two fingers between balls. The balls grow while they rise, and they must not stick together.

**Size:** about {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.container_l.value }} litre for each ball. The dough must have room to double without touching the lid.

### Cover

The goal is to keep moving air off the dough. Air makes the surface dry and crusty. A dry skin tears when you shape the dough, and it does not puff. From best to worst:

- **A container with a plain lid.** The lid does not need to be airtight. It only has to stop the air from blowing on the dough. A tight lid is also fine, because the dough makes only a little gas in the fridge.
- **An open container with a plate or plastic wrap** over it. Pull the wrap tight around the rim.
- **A damp towel**, only if you have nothing else and only for a short time (at most {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.towel_max_min.value }} minutes). Never lay it directly on the dough, because it sticks and dries out. Put it over a plate or the container.

### Time and temperature

- **Room temperature of {{ recipes.oven.room_c.value }} degrees** is good for the time the dough stays out. If the room is warmer than {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.hot_room_c.value }} degrees, keep the time out shorter.
- **Fridge:** {{ recipes.methods.cold_24h.fridge_h.value }} hours is good, and {{ recipes.methods.cold_48h.fridge_h.value }} hours gives more flavour. {{ recipes.methods.cold_72h.fridge_h.value }} hours is the longest this guide uses.

### Is the dough ready?

- **Warm method:** after the first rise the dough is {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.rise_factor.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.rise_factor.range.1 }} times bigger, and the surface is soft and puffy.
- **Cold fermentation:** the balls are a little bigger and you can see small bubbles at the bottom of the container. Press the dough gently with a finger. If it springs back slowly, it is ready. If it springs back at once, it needs more time. If your finger leaves a dent that does not come back, the dough has risen too much (see Troubleshooting).

## Shaping

### Making dough balls

1. Pull the edges of the dough to the middle, turn it over, and roll it with your palm on the table until the surface is tight and smooth.
   > Why: A tight surface keeps the gas in, and the ball opens more easily later.

### Opening the dough

2. Sprinkle about {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.bench_flour_g.value }} g of flour or semolina on the table (more on semolina below).
3. Put the ball on it and press from the middle outwards with your fingertips. **Leave a rim of {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.rim_cm.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.rim_cm.range.1 }} cm untouched.**
4. Stretch the dough with both hands and turn it until it is about {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.shape_diameter_cm.value }} cm wide.
   > Why: The untouched rim keeps its air and puffs in the oven. This is how you get a rim with air in it.
5. Put the dough on baking paper, or on the floured peel or a board if you bake without paper (see Oven and baking surface).

> Warning: Do not use a rolling pin. It presses the air out of the dough, and the rim does not puff.

> Tip: Work fast once the dough is open. Dough that waits on the paper for a long time sticks to it.

- **The dough springs back:** the gluten is tight. Rest it for {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.shrink_rest_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.shrink_rest_min.range.1 }} minutes.
- **The dough tears:** it is cold or the gluten is weak. Rest it, or patch the hole with a small piece of dough.

### What is semolina for?

Semolina is a coarse flour from durum wheat, which is hard wheat. The grains feel like fine sand. It is optional.

- Its coarse grains do not soak up water from the dough as fast as normal flour, so the dough does not turn pasty.
- The grains work like tiny rollers, so the dough slides easily on the board and onto the hot tray.
- It adds a little crispness to the base.

Normal flour also works. Use a thin layer and shake off the extra, because too much flour burns on a hot tray.

## Oven and baking surface

The baking surface stores heat. When the dough lands on it, the surface gives its heat to the base quickly, so the base turns crisp and the dough puffs fast. A pizza stone, a baking steel or a cast-iron pan all work. Steel and cast iron pass on heat better than stone. The cast-iron advice here is home-tested, not tested in a controlled way.

1. **Heat the surface for {{ recipes.oven.preheat_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.oven.preheat_min.range.1 }} minutes before baking.** Put the stone, steel or tray in the oven and set the oven to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.bake.temp_c.value }} degrees.
   > Why: A surface that is not fully hot gives a pale, soft base.
2. **Put it on the upper rack** (about the upper third of the oven).
3. **Use top and bottom heat** and **no fan**. If your oven has a fan mode and the top browns too fast, switch the fan off.
   > Why: The fan dries the surface of the dough and burns the top before the base is ready.
4. **Move the pizza onto the hot surface fast.** Slide it, with the paper if you use paper, from a board or peel onto the surface. Cut off extra paper at the edges.
   > Why: Every time the oven door opens, heat is lost. Be quick.
5. **Bake for {{ recipes.styles.new_york.bake.time_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.bake.time_min.range.1 }} minutes.** The pizza is done when the rim is golden to light brown and the base is crisp.
6. **For the last {{ recipes.styles.new_york.bake.grill.last_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.bake.grill.last_min.range.1 }} minutes turn on the grill** to colour the top.
   > Why: A {{ recipes.styles.new_york.bake.temp_c.value }} degree oven is short on heat from above.
7. **Second pizza:** keep its dough covered until the first pizza is out and the surface is hot again, which takes about {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.second_pizza_wait_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.second_pizza_wait_min.range.1 }} minutes. Only then open it.
   > Why: Dough that waits on the paper gets wet and sticks.

> Safety: Many baking papers are rated to {{ recipes.safety.parchment_max_c.value }} degrees, which is below this oven. Never put it under the grill. The full note is in the bake step of your method.

> Safety: Extra-virgin olive oil starts to smoke at about {{ recipes.safety.evoo_smoke_c.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.safety.evoo_smoke_c.range.1 }} degrees. Do not oil the hot surface or pan. Oil in the dough, and a thin film on the rim, is fine.

## Bake-day checklist, warm method {methods=warm_2h}

- **Hour 0:** yeast, lukewarm water, flour and salt. Then sugar and oil. Knead for {{ recipes.methods.warm_2h.knead_min.value }} minutes.
- **First rise:** {{ recipes.methods.warm_2h.bulk_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.methods.warm_2h.bulk_min.range.1 }} minutes in a warm place.
- **Then:** divide into two and shape the balls. Switch on the oven and the tray.
- **Rest the balls:** {{ recipes.methods.warm_2h.final_rest_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.methods.warm_2h.final_rest_min.range.1 }} minutes, covered.

## Troubleshooting

- **The base is pale or stays soft.** The surface was not hot enough. Heat the surface for {{ recipes.oven.preheat_min.range.1 }} minutes.
- **The dough springs back.** The gluten is tight, or the dough is cold. Rest it for {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.cold_dough_rest_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.cold_dough_rest_min.range.1 }} minutes.
- **The dough is very sticky.** See the Kneading section, "The dough is sticky".
- **The dough is very stiff.** See the Kneading section, "The dough is dry".
- **A sharp smell of alcohol or sourness.** The dough rose too much. Next time use less yeast or less time.
- **The rim did not puff.** You pressed the rim when you shaped it, or the dough had not risen enough.
- **The dough tears.** Rest it. If it is cold, leave it out longer.
- **The dough surface is dry and crusty.** The cover was not good (see Container, oil and cover).
- **The top is raw but the base is browned.** Move the rack higher and use the grill for the last {{ recipes.styles.new_york.bake.grill.last_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.bake.grill.last_min.range.1 }} minutes.
- **The base burned.** Move the rack one step down.
- **You used too much yeast.** The dough is usually still usable, but it has risen too much. Keep it out of the fridge for only {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.over_yeast_out_min.value }} hour, do not knead the air out (just open it with your fingertips), and bake fast. Next time use the amount from the calculator.
