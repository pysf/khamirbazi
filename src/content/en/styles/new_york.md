## The finished pizza

A New York pizza is thin to medium in the middle, with a crisp base that still bends. You can fold a slice in half. All temperatures on this site are in degrees Celsius. Here is what to expect:

- **Base:** crisp, but it bends. It does not snap like a cracker.
- **Rim (the edge of the crust):** medium in size. It is not flat like a cracker and not puffed up like a Neapolitan rim.
- **Crumb (the inside of the crust):** chewy.
- **Browning:** the surface turns golden to light brown. Sugar and protein react in the heat, and this adds a toasted, slightly sweet taste.
- **At home:** a {{ recipes.oven.max_c.value }} degree oven makes the rim a little more like bread than in a pizza restaurant. The base still turns crisp on a hot baking surface.

## Choosing a fermentation

There are two ways to ferment this dough. Both give a good pizza. They differ in time and taste. You choose the fermentation on the Home page, and the Ingredients card on this page shows the amounts for it. To try another fermentation, press "Change" at the top of the page.

**Warm fermentation, 2 hours: when you are in a hurry.** You mix the dough and bake about two hours later.

- **How it works:** more yeast, lukewarm water and {{ recipes.methods.warm_2h.knead_min.value }} minutes of kneading. The yeast works fast, so the dough rises in about an hour.
- **Pros:** ready the same day. No planning.
- **Cons:** the taste is simpler, more like plain bread. The browning is a little weaker.
- **Needs at least {{ recipes.methods.warm_2h.min_pizzas }} pizzas.** For one pizza the dry yeast would weigh less than a kitchen scale can show, and this guide keeps the same rule for fresh yeast.

**Cold fermentation: when you can wait until tomorrow.** Fermentation is the yeast eating sugar and making gas and flavour. In cold fermentation the dough does this slowly in the fridge (about {{ recipes.oven.fridge_c.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.oven.fridge_c.range.1 }} degrees). You can choose from {{ recipes.methods.cold_12_16h.fridge_h.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.methods.cold_12_16h.fridge_h.range.1 }} hours (overnight) up to {{ recipes.methods.cold_72h.fridge_h.value }} hours.

- **How it works:** very little yeast, cold water and lighter kneading. In the cold the yeast works slowly, and the enzymes in the flour have time to break down starch and protein.
- **Pros:** deeper flavour, better browning and a lighter, chewier rim. On baking day there is little to do: take the dough out, shape it and bake. If your plans change, the dough keeps in the fridge about a day longer.
- **Cons:** you must plan ahead, the dough needs fridge space, and the yeast amount has to be exact. That is why cold fermentation usually uses a yeast solution (the Ingredients card tells you when).

**In short:** if you have until tomorrow, choose cold fermentation. If you want to eat today, warm fermentation still makes a tasty pizza.

## Flour and yeast

**Flour.** Use pizza flour or strong bread flour. Pizza flour often says Tipo 00 on the pack. Tipo 00 is the most refined Italian flour grade; it is very fine and white.

- **Check the protein.** Find the line "protein" in the nutrition table. The number is per 100 g of flour. A good value is {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.flour_protein_g.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.flour_protein_g.range.1 }} g. Read the number on your pack, because flours differ.
- **Why more protein is better:** more protein means stronger gluten. Gluten is the stretchy net that holds the gas from the yeast. Stronger dough stretches better, tears less when you shape it, and handles a long time in the fridge better.
- **High-gluten flour:** flour with {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.high_gluten_protein_g.value }} g or more is classic for New York. The dough is stronger and springs back more, so give it longer rests when you shape it.

**Yeast.** Use the yeast you have. The Ingredients card shows the yeast you chose on the Home page (dry or fresh).

- **Dry yeast:** a common packet holds {{ recipes.yeast.solution.dry.yeast_g.value }} g (check your pack). Most packets hold instant yeast. You can stir it straight into the flour or the water, with no waiting. Read the text on your packet to be sure. Close an opened packet tightly.
- **Fresh yeast:** a small soft cube, commonly {{ recipes.yeast.solution.fresh.cube_g.value }} g (check your pack), sold in the fridge section. It does not keep long. Wrap what is left and keep it in the fridge.
- **Equivalent:** use {{ recipes.yeast.fresh_to_dry.value }} times as much fresh yeast as dry yeast.

> Warning: The yeast amounts are a starting point, not a fixed rule. Sources differ by up to {{ recipes.yeast.source_spread_x.value }} times for the same schedule. The right amount depends on your fridge temperature and your yeast. Bake once, look at the dough and the crust, and change the amount next time.

## Warm fermentation, 2 hours {methods=warm_2h}

You mix the dough and bake about two hours later. Take the amounts from the Ingredients card on this page.

> Warning: Warm fermentation needs at least {{ recipes.methods.warm_2h.min_pizzas }} pizzas. For one pizza the dry yeast would weigh less than {{ recipes.yeast.min_weigh_g.value }} g, and a kitchen scale cannot show that. This guide keeps the same rule for fresh yeast. For one pizza, use cold fermentation.

1. **Prepare the yeast**
   1. Put the dough water, lukewarm at about {{ recipes.methods.warm_2h.water_temp_c.value }} degrees, in a large bowl.
   2. Weigh the yeast amount shown in the table (the Dry yeast or Fresh yeast row).
   3. Crumble fresh yeast first, then stir the yeast into the water. Instant dry yeast needs no waiting.
   > Why: Yeast works fast between {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.yeast_works_c.range.0 }} and {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.yeast_works_c.range.1 }} degrees and starts to die from about {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.yeast_dies_c.value }} degrees, so never use hot water. Lukewarm water feels just slightly warm on your wrist, cooler than a bath.

   > Tip: If your yeast is old, or you are not sure it is alive, check it first. Stir it into the lukewarm water with a pinch of sugar and wait {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.yeast_check_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.yeast_check_min.range.1 }} minutes. It should foam. If there is no foam, use new yeast.
2. **Mix the dough**
   1. Add about half of the flour to the yeast water and stir until it looks like a thick batter.
   2. Add the rest of the flour in two or three portions, and add the salt with the last portion.
   3. Stir until you see no dry flour.
   > Why: Flour added in portions gets wet evenly, so you get no dry lumps. The salt goes in with the flour, not on the yeast, because salt in direct contact with yeast slows it down.

   > Tip: If the dough feels dry or stiff, wet your hand and work in a little water (see the Kneading section, "The dough is dry").
3. **Knead**
   Add the sugar and the olive oil, then knead for {{ recipes.methods.warm_2h.knead_min.value }} minutes. See the Kneading section below for how to knead.
   > Why: There is little time, so kneading has to build the gluten that a long rest would build.
4. **Let the dough rise**
   1. Put the dough in an oiled container with a lid (see the section Container, oil and cover). The container must be big enough for the dough to grow.
   2. Leave it in a warm place for {{ recipes.methods.warm_2h.bulk_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.methods.warm_2h.bulk_min.range.1 }} minutes. A good temperature is {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.warm_place_c.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.warm_place_c.range.1 }} degrees.
   3. The dough should grow to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.rise_factor.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.rise_factor.range.1 }} times its size, with a soft, puffy surface. If you have no warm place, the rise can take up to {{ recipes.methods.warm_2h.bulk_slow_max_min.value }} minutes: wait for the size, not the clock.
   > Why: The yeast makes gas, and the gas makes the dough bigger and puffier. The air in the dough makes the rim puff in the oven. The cover keeps the surface from drying into a skin.

   > Tip: Mark the level of the dough on the outside of the container with tape or a pen. Then you can see how much it has grown.
5. **Heat the oven and the baking surface**
   Do this about halfway through the rise, so the surface is hot when the balls are ready (see the section Oven and baking surface).
   1. If the dough rises in the oven, take it out first.
   2. Put the baking surface on the upper rack and turn the oven on.
   > Why: The baking surface needs {{ recipes.oven.preheat_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.oven.preheat_min.range.1 }} minutes to get hot, so the oven has to start before the balls are ready.
6. **Divide and ball**
   1. Weigh the whole dough on the scale. It should be close to Total dough in the table.
   2. Divide the weight by the number of pizzas and cut off equal pieces of that weight.
   3. Shape each into a ball (see the section Shaping). Keep them covered.
   > Why: Equal balls bake evenly and make pizzas of the same size. A scale is more accurate than your eye.
7. **Rest the balls**
   Keep the balls covered for {{ recipes.methods.warm_2h.final_rest_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.methods.warm_2h.final_rest_min.range.1 }} minutes. The oven should now be heating (you turned it on in the step Heat the oven and the baking surface).
   > Why: The gluten relaxes. A relaxed ball stretches easily and does not shrink back. The cover keeps the balls from drying out.
8. **Open the dough**
   1. Follow the section Shaping.
   2. Dust the table lightly and stretch the first ball gently so it does not tear.
   3. Keep the other balls covered.
   > Tip: If a big bubble forms in the flat middle of the dough, pop it with a fingertip. Leave the rim alone, so it keeps its air.
9. **Dress the pizza**
   1. Spread a thin layer of sauce and leave the rim bare (about {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.rim_cm.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.rim_cm.range.1 }} cm).
   2. Add cheese and any toppings you like.
   3. Brush the rim lightly with olive oil. Work fast, because dough that waits on the board or paper sticks.
   > Why: Light toppings let the steam escape, so the base turns crisp. Heavy or wet toppings keep it soft. Pat wet toppings dry first, and use dry grated cheese.

   > Tip: A small pinch of garlic powder on top of the cheese tastes good. It is optional. Garlic powder burns less than fresh garlic.
10. **Slide the pizza in**
    Slide the pizza onto the hot baking surface from a board or peel (a flat paddle for pizza).
    > Tip: Before you bake, check that the baking surface has had its full {{ recipes.oven.preheat_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.oven.preheat_min.range.1 }} minutes in the hot oven. A surface that is not fully hot gives a pale, soft base.

    > Safety: Baking paper is your choice. Without paper, open the dough on a board or peel dusted with a little semolina or flour, then slide it onto the hot surface. With paper, check the maximum temperature printed on the pack. Many papers are rated to {{ recipes.safety.parchment_max_c.value }} degrees, which is lower than this oven. Keep the paper away from the heating elements and the oven walls, and trim the edges. Do not use wax paper. If your paper is rated below the oven temperature, pull it out from under the pizza after the first {{ recipes.safety.parchment_pull_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.safety.parchment_pull_min.range.1 }} minutes, when the base has set. If you use paper, skip the grill unless the pack says it is safe.

    > Safety: Extra-virgin olive oil starts to smoke at about {{ recipes.safety.evoo_smoke_c.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.safety.evoo_smoke_c.range.1 }} degrees, and this oven is at {{ recipes.styles.new_york.bake.temp_c.value }} degrees. Do not oil the hot baking surface. Oil inside the dough is fine. For the rim, use only a thin film, and choose refined or light olive oil because it smokes less.

    > Safety: Use oven gloves. Pull the rack out only a little to slide the pizza on, and never touch the baking surface; it stays hot for a long time after the oven is off.
11. **Bake**
    Bake for {{ recipes.styles.new_york.bake.time_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.bake.time_min.range.1 }} minutes. Follow the section Oven and baking surface.
12. **Rest and cut**
    Take the pizza out and let it rest for {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.rest_before_cut_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.rest_before_cut_min.range.1 }} minutes before you cut it. Leave it uncovered, on a rack if you have one. While it rests, add fresh or delicate extras: a drizzle of olive oil, fresh herbs such as basil, grated hard cheese, chili flakes, or a squeeze of lemon on some toppings.
    > Why: Steam escapes and the cheese sets, so the toppings do not slide off. An uncovered base stays crisp. Fresh or delicate extras go on after baking so they do not burn or wilt.


> Tip: A switched-off oven with the light on is a good warm place. Take the dough out before you turn the oven on: a plastic container or lid melts in a heating oven.

## Cold fermentation {methods=cold_12_16h,cold_24h,cold_48h,cold_72h}

You make the dough one or more days before baking. Take the amounts from the Ingredients card on this page. It shows the amounts for the time you chose on the Home page. The steps up to Chill the dough are the first day. The steps from Take the dough out are baking day.

1. **Prepare the yeast**
   1. Pour the cold water (about {{ recipes.methods.cold_24h.water_temp_c.value }} degrees) into a large bowl.
   2. Add the yeast like this:
      - **If the table has a Yeast solution row:** in a separate cup, stir one whole {{ recipes.yeast.solution.dry.yeast_g.value }} g packet of dry yeast (or half of a {{ recipes.yeast.solution.fresh.cube_g.value }} g cube of fresh yeast) into {{ recipes.yeast.solution.dry.water_g.value }} g of lukewarm water ({{ recipes.yeast.solution.water_temp_c.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.yeast.solution.water_temp_c.range.1 }} degrees) until no lumps are left. Wait {{ recipes.yeast.solution.dissolve_wait_min.value }} minutes and stir again. Then pour the amount shown in the table into the dough water right away, and throw the rest away.
      - **If the table has a Dry yeast or Fresh yeast row:** weigh that amount and stir it into the water. Crumble fresh yeast first. Instant dry yeast needs no waiting.
   > Why: A small amount of yeast is too light for a kitchen scale, so you weigh part of a yeast solution instead. A larger batch has enough yeast to weigh directly. The solution row in the table already has its weight taken off the cold water.

   > Tip: If your yeast is old, or you are not sure it is alive, check it first in a separate cup. Stir it into lukewarm water with a pinch of sugar and wait {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.yeast_check_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.yeast_check_min.range.1 }} minutes. It should foam. If there is no foam, use new yeast.
2. **Mix the dough**
   1. Add about half of the flour and stir.
   2. Then add the rest in two or three portions.
   3. Stir only until you see no dry flour. The dough will be cool, not cold; that is fine.
   > Why: Flour added in portions gets wet evenly. The salt comes later, so it does not touch the yeast alone.
3. **Rest the dough**
   Cover the bowl and leave the dough for {{ recipes.methods.cold_24h.autolyse_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.methods.cold_24h.autolyse_min.range.1 }} minutes. This rest is called autolyse.
   > Why: The water and the enzymes in the flour start to build gluten by themselves, so you need less kneading.
4. **Add the salt and knead**
   1. Add the salt, the sugar and the olive oil, and work them into the dough until it is even.
   2. Then knead for {{ recipes.methods.cold_24h.knead_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.methods.cold_24h.knead_min.range.1 }} minutes (see the section Kneading).
   - If you prefer stretch and fold, skip the kneading and go to the step Let the dough rise.
   > Why: Salt makes the gluten stronger and slows the yeast down.

   > Tip: If the dough feels dry or stiff, wet your hand and work in a little water (see the Kneading section, "The dough is dry").
5. **Let the dough rise**
   1. - Put the dough in a container with a lid, big enough for the dough to grow.
   2. Leave it, covered, at about {{ recipes.oven.room_c.value }} degrees for {{ recipes.methods.cold_24h.room_rest_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.methods.cold_24h.room_rest_min.range.1 }} minutes, until it is about {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.cold_rise_pct.value }}% bigger.
   - If you chose stretch and fold, do it during this rise: {{ recipes.methods.cold_24h.stretch_fold.rounds.value }} rounds. Do the first round now and the next ones {{ recipes.methods.cold_24h.stretch_fold.gap_min.value }} minutes apart. After the last round, leave the dough alone until the rise is over. Let the rise run the full {{ recipes.methods.cold_24h.room_rest_min.range.1 }} minutes.
   > Why: The yeast needs a short warm start before it goes into the cold. Stretch and fold makes the dough strong while it rises, so the rounds and the rise happen in the same time. The cover keeps the surface from drying into a skin.

   > Tip: Mark the level of the dough on the outside of the container with tape or a pen. Then you can see how much it has grown.
6. **Divide and ball**
   1. Weigh the whole dough on the scale. It should be close to Total dough in the table.
   2. Divide the weight by the number of pizzas and cut off equal pieces of that weight.
   3. Shape each into a ball (see the section Shaping). Put each ball in its own oiled container with a lid (see the section Container, oil and cover).
7. **Chill the dough**
   Put the containers in the fridge for the time you chose: {{ recipes.methods.cold_12_16h.fridge_h.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.methods.cold_12_16h.fridge_h.range.1 }} hours (overnight), {{ recipes.methods.cold_24h.fridge_h.value }} hours, {{ recipes.methods.cold_48h.fridge_h.value }} hours or {{ recipes.methods.cold_72h.fridge_h.value }} hours. The yeast amount in the table is for that time. To change the time, press "Change" at the top of the page. {{ recipes.methods.cold_72h.fridge_h.value }} hours is the longest this guide uses.
   > Why: In the fridge the yeast works slowly, and the enzymes have time to build flavour. The result is a deeper taste and better browning.
8. **Take the dough out of the fridge**
   On baking day, take the containers out of the fridge {{ recipes.methods.cold_24h.take_out_lead_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.methods.cold_24h.take_out_lead_min.range.1 }} minutes before you shape the dough. Keep the lids closed.
   > Why: Cold dough does not stretch well and it tears. This is the most common mistake with cold dough.
9. **Heat the oven and the baking surface**
   Put the baking surface on the upper rack and turn the oven on {{ recipes.oven.preheat_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.oven.preheat_min.range.1 }} minutes before baking, while the dough warms up (see the section Oven and baking surface).
10. **Open the dough**
    1. Follow the section Shaping.
    2. Dust the table lightly and stretch the first ball gently so it does not tear.
    3. Keep the other balls covered.
    > Tip: If a big bubble forms in the flat middle of the dough, pop it with a fingertip. Leave the rim alone, so it keeps its air.
11. **Dress the pizza**
    1. Spread a thin layer of sauce and leave the rim bare (about {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.rim_cm.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.rim_cm.range.1 }} cm).
    2. Add cheese and any toppings you like.
    3. Brush the rim lightly with olive oil. Work fast, because dough that waits on the board or paper sticks.
    > Why: Light toppings let the steam escape, so the base turns crisp. Heavy or wet toppings keep it soft. Pat wet toppings dry first, and use dry grated cheese.

    Slide the pizza onto the hot baking surface from a board or peel (a flat paddle for pizza).
    > Tip: Before you bake, check that the baking surface has had its full {{ recipes.oven.preheat_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.oven.preheat_min.range.1 }} minutes in the hot oven. A surface that is not fully hot gives a pale, soft base.

    > Safety: Baking paper is your choice. Without paper, open the dough on a board or peel dusted with a little semolina or flour, then slide it onto the hot surface. ## Kneading

Kneading means pulling, pressing and folding the dough to build gluten, the stretchy net that holds the gas. The goal is to turn a sticky, lumpy mass into a smooth, soft ball.

### By hand ({{ recipes.methods.cold_24h.knead_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.methods.cold_24h.knead_min.range.1 }} minutes)

1. Put the dough on a clean table. **Do not add extra flour to the table.**
   > Why: Extra flour makes the dough stiff.
2. Press the dough away from you with the heel of your hand, and stretch it.
3. Fold the far edge over the dough.
4. Turn the dough a quarter turn.
5. Repeat these four moves (press, stretch, fold, turn) until the time is up.

### How the dough feels

- **Minutes {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.kneading_early_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.kneading_early_min.range.1 }}:** lumpy and very sticky. It sticks to your hands and the table. This is normal.
- **Minutes {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.kneading_mid_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.kneading_mid_min.range.1 }}:** less sticky. The dough starts to hold together.
- **Minutes {{ recipes.methods.cold_24h.knead_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.methods.cold_24h.knead_min.range.1 }}:** smooth, soft and stretchy. It still feels a little sticky, but a clean, dry hand does not pick up dough. When you press it with a finger, it springs back slowly.

### Windowpane test

Take a small piece of dough and stretch it slowly between your fingers. If it turns thin and see-through without tearing, the gluten is strong enough. If it tears fast, knead for {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.windowpane_extra_knead_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.windowpane_extra_knead_min.range.1 }} more minutes, or rest the dough for {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.windowpane_rest_min.value }} minutes.

### The dough is sticky

The dough has {{ recipes.styles.new_york.dough.hydration.value }}% water, so it is soft and a little sticky. At first you will want to add flour. **Do not.** Extra flour makes the dough stiff and dense. Do this instead:

- Wet your hands a little. Keep a bowl of water next to you.
- Use a plastic scraper to lift the dough from the table.
- Rest the dough for {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.sticky_rest_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.sticky_rest_min.range.1 }} minutes, then knead again. The gluten gets stronger while the dough rests, and it becomes less sticky.
- If the dough is still as loose as cake batter after {{ recipes.methods.cold_24h.knead_min.range.1 }} minutes of kneading, and it sticks to a clean hand: rest it, covered, then do {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.sticky_fold_rounds.range.0 }} or {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.sticky_fold_rounds.range.1 }} rounds of stretch and fold, {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.sticky_fold_gap_min.value }} minutes apart. Only if it is still very loose, add {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.sticky_add_flour_g.value }} g of flour and knead well. Never add more than {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.sticky_max_flour_g.value }} g of flour in total.

### The dough is dry

If the dough is stiff, cracks or tires your hands:

- Wet your hand and add {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.dry_add_water_g.value }} g of water. The dough turns slippery first, but the water soaks in as you knead.
- Rest it for {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.dry_rest_min.value }} minutes.
- If it is still stiff, add {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.dry_add_water_g.value }} g of water again. Never add more than {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.dry_max_water_g.value }} g in total.
- Common causes: a flour with more protein soaks up more water, the water or flour was not weighed exactly, or the air in the room is dry.

### Stretch and fold

Use this when kneading by hand is hard or the dough is loose. One round looks like this:

1. Wet your hand.
2. Take one side of the dough and pull it up gently, without tearing it.
3. Fold it over the middle of the dough.
4. Turn the bowl a quarter turn and repeat with the next side.
5. Keep going until you have pulled and folded all four sides. That is one round.

Cover the dough between the rounds.

### With a mixer

Use low speed for {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.machine_knead_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.machine_knead_min.range.1 }} minutes. The dough should come off the bowl wall and gather around the hook. Do not mix for longer, because the machine warms the dough.

## Container, oil and cover

### Oiled container

An oiled container keeps the dough from sticking, so it comes out without tearing. It also keeps the surface from drying.

1. Use about {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.oil_per_container_g.value }} g of liquid oil (olive oil or another liquid oil) for each container.
2. Wipe a **thin layer** over the bottom and the walls with a finger or kitchen paper. No oil should pool at the bottom.
3. Put the dough or ball in and turn it once, so the top has a little oil too.
4. Give each ball its own container, or leave a gap of two fingers between balls. The balls grow while they rise, and they must not stick together.

**Size:** about {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.container_l.value }} litre for each ball. The dough must have room to double without touching the lid.

### Cover

Moving air dries the surface of the dough and makes it crusty. A dry skin tears when you shape the dough, and it does not puff. So cover the dough. From best to worst:

- **A container with a plain lid.** The lid does not need to be airtight. It only has to stop air from blowing on the dough. A tight lid is also fine, because the dough makes only a little gas in the fridge.
- **An open container with a plate or plastic wrap** over it. Pull the wrap tight around the rim.
- **A damp towel**, only if you have nothing else and only for a short time (at most {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.towel_max_min.value }} minutes). Never lay it directly on the dough, because it sticks and dries out. Put it over a plate or the container.

### Time and temperature

- **Room temperature of {{ recipes.oven.room_c.value }} degrees** is good for the time the dough stays out. If the room is warmer than {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.hot_room_c.value }} degrees, keep the dough out for a shorter time.
- **Fridge:** {{ recipes.methods.cold_24h.fridge_h.value }} hours is good, and {{ recipes.methods.cold_48h.fridge_h.value }} hours gives more flavour. {{ recipes.methods.cold_72h.fridge_h.value }} hours is the longest this guide uses.

### Is the dough ready?

- **Warm fermentation:** after the first rise the dough is {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.rise_factor.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.rise_factor.range.1 }} times bigger, and the surface is soft and puffy.
- **Cold fermentation:** the balls are a little bigger and you can see small bubbles at the bottom of the container. Press the dough gently with a finger. If it springs back slowly, it is ready. If it springs back at once, it needs more time. If your finger leaves a dent that does not come back, the dough has risen too much (see Troubleshooting).

## Shaping

### Making dough balls

1. Pull the edges of the dough to the middle, turn it over, and roll it with your palm on the table until the surface is tight and smooth.
   > Why: A tight surface keeps the gas in, and the ball opens more easily later.

### Opening the dough

Opening means stretching a ball into a flat round.

1. Sprinkle about {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.bench_flour_g.value }} g of flour or semolina on the table (more on semolina below).
2. Put the ball on it and press from the middle outwards with your fingertips. **Leave a rim of {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.rim_cm.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.rim_cm.range.1 }} cm untouched.**
3. Stretch the dough with both hands and turn it until it is about the size you chose on the Home page (it is shown at the top of this page).
   > Why: The untouched rim keeps its air, so it puffs in the oven.
4. Put the dough on a board or peel dusted with semolina or flour, or on baking paper if you prefer it (see the section Oven and baking surface).

> Warning: Do not use a rolling pin. It presses the air out of the dough, and the rim does not puff.

> Tip: Work fast once the dough is open. Dough that waits on the board or paper for a long time sticks to it.

- **The dough springs back:** the gluten is tight. Rest it for {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.shrink_rest_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.shrink_rest_min.range.1 }} minutes.
- **The dough tears:** it is cold or the gluten is weak. Rest it, or patch the hole with a small piece of dough.

### What is semolina for?

Semolina is a coarse flour from durum wheat, which is hard wheat. The grains feel like fine sand. It is optional.

- Its coarse grains do not soak up water from the dough as fast as normal flour, so the dough does not turn pasty.
- The grains work like tiny rollers, so the dough slides easily on the board and onto the hot baking surface.
- It adds a little crispness to the base.

Normal flour also works. Use a thin layer and shake off the extra, because too much flour burns on a hot baking surface.

## Oven and baking surface

The baking surface is a pizza stone, a baking steel or a cast-iron pan. Use the one you have. It stores heat. When the dough lands on it, the surface gives its heat to the base quickly, so the base turns crisp and the dough puffs fast. Steel and cast iron pass on heat better than stone. The advice about cast iron is home-tested, not tested in a controlled way.

1. **Place the baking surface**
   Put the baking surface on the upper rack (about the upper third of the oven). Do this before you turn the oven on.
2. **Preheat the surface**
   Heat the baking surface for {{ recipes.oven.preheat_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.oven.preheat_min.range.1 }} minutes before baking. Set the oven to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.bake.temp_c.value }} degrees.
   > Why: A surface that is not fully hot gives a pale, soft base.
3. **Set the heat**
   Use top and bottom heat and no fan. If your oven has a fan mode, switch the fan off.
   > Why: Top and bottom heat sends heat straight into the baking surface from below. In fan mode the bottom element is often off, and the moving air dries and browns the top faster than the base. If your oven has a pizza mode (fan with bottom heat), you can try it and watch the top.
4. **Move the pizza on**
   Slide the pizza from a board or peel onto the hot surface fast, with the paper if you use paper (then trim the edges).
   > Why: Every time you open the oven door, heat is lost. Be quick.
5. **Bake**
   Bake for {{ recipes.styles.new_york.bake.time_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.bake.time_min.range.1 }} minutes. On a pizza stone it can take up to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.bake.stone_max_min.value }} minutes. The pizza is done when the rim is golden to light brown and the base is crisp.
6. **Use the grill**
   For the last {{ recipes.styles.new_york.bake.grill.last_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.bake.grill.last_min.range.1 }} minutes, turn on the grill to colour the top. Skip this if you use baking paper, unless the pack says it is safe.
   > Why: A {{ recipes.styles.new_york.bake.temp_c.value }} degree oven gives too little heat from above.
7. **Wait for a second pizza**
   Keep its ball covered until the first pizza is out and the surface is hot again. This takes about {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.second_pizza_wait_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.second_pizza_wait_min.range.1 }} minutes. Only then open the ball.
   > Why: Dough that waits on the board or paper gets wet and sticks.

> Safety: Baking paper is your choice. If you use it, check the maximum temperature printed on the pack (many papers are rated to {{ recipes.safety.parchment_max_c.value }} degrees, which is lower than this oven) and trim the edges. Do not use wax paper. The full note is in the bake step of your fermentation.

> Safety: Extra-virgin olive oil starts to smoke at about {{ recipes.safety.evoo_smoke_c.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.safety.evoo_smoke_c.range.1 }} degrees. Do not oil the hot baking surface. Oil in the dough, and a thin film on the rim, is fine.

> Safety: Use oven gloves. Pull the rack out only a little to slide the pizza on, and never touch the baking surface; it stays hot for a long time after the oven is off.

## Bake-day checklist, warm fermentation {methods=warm_2h}

- **Hour 0:** mix the yeast, lukewarm water, flour and salt. Then add the sugar and oil. Knead for {{ recipes.methods.warm_2h.knead_min.value }} minutes.
- **First rise:** {{ recipes.methods.warm_2h.bulk_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.methods.warm_2h.bulk_min.range.1 }} minutes in a warm place (up to {{ recipes.methods.warm_2h.bulk_slow_max_min.value }} minutes without one). About halfway through, take the dough out if it rises in the oven, and turn on the oven with the baking surface inside.
- **Then:** weigh the whole dough, divide the weight by the number of pizzas, and shape equal balls.
- **Rest the balls:** {{ recipes.methods.warm_2h.final_rest_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.methods.warm_2h.final_rest_min.range.1 }} minutes, covered. The oven keeps heating.
- **About {{ recipes.timeline.shape_lead_min.value }} minutes before baking:** open the dough, dress it and bake.

## Bake-day checklist, cold fermentation {methods=cold_12_16h,cold_24h,cold_48h,cold_72h}

- **{{ recipes.methods.cold_24h.take_out_lead_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.methods.cold_24h.take_out_lead_min.range.1 }} minutes before shaping:** take the dough out of the fridge and keep the lid closed.
- **{{ recipes.oven.preheat_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.oven.preheat_min.range.1 }} minutes before baking:** set the oven to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.bake.temp_c.value }} degrees, with the baking surface on the upper rack.
- **{{ recipes.timeline.shape_lead_min.value }} minutes before baking:** dust the table, open the dough and dress it.
- **Work fast** and move the pizza onto the hot surface.
- **Bake** for {{ recipes.styles.new_york.bake.time_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.bake.time_min.range.1 }} minutes (up to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.bake.stone_max_min.value }} on a stone), with the grill for the last {{ recipes.styles.new_york.bake.grill.last_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.bake.grill.last_min.range.1 }} minutes.

## Troubleshooting

- **The base is pale or stays soft.** The surface was not hot enough. Heat it for {{ recipes.oven.preheat_min.range.1 }} minutes.
- **The dough springs back.** The gluten is tight, or the dough is cold. Rest it for {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.cold_dough_rest_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.cold_dough_rest_min.range.1 }} minutes.
- **The dough is very sticky.** See the Kneading section, "The dough is sticky".
- **The dough is very stiff.** See the Kneading section, "The dough is dry".
- **A sharp smell of alcohol or sourness.** The dough rose too much. Next time use less yeast or less time.
- **The rim did not puff.** You pressed the rim when you shaped it, or the dough had not risen enough.
- **The dough tears.** Rest it. If it is cold, leave it out of the fridge for longer.
- **The dough surface is dry and crusty.** The cover was not good enough (see Container, oil and cover).
- **The top is pale but the base is browned.** Move the rack higher and use the grill for the last {{ recipes.styles.new_york.bake.grill.last_min.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.bake.grill.last_min.range.1 }} minutes.
- **The base burned.** Move the rack one step higher, or bake a minute less. With steel or cast iron, heat the surface for only {{ recipes.oven.preheat_min.range.0 }} minutes.
- **You used too much yeast.** The dough is usually still usable, but it has risen too much. After you take it out of the fridge, let it warm up for only {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.over_yeast_out_min.value }} minutes. Do not knead the air out. Just open it with your fingertips, and bake fast. Next time use the amount from the calculator.
