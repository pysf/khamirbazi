## Hydration

Hydration is the weight of water as a percentage of the weight of flour. The New York dough in this guide has {{ recipes.styles.new_york.dough.hydration.value }}% hydration: that many grams of water for every 100 g of flour. All temperatures on this site are in degrees Celsius.

- Stiffer dough is easy to handle and bakes crisp.
- Softer, stickier dough gives an open, airy crumb (the inside of the crust) but is harder to handle.

This guide keeps the hydration moderate. A very wet dough is hard for a beginner to handle and browns slowly. A very stiff dough can turn dry and hard in a long home-oven bake.

## Gluten

Gluten is a stretchy network that forms when the proteins in flour meet water. It holds the gas the yeast makes, so the dough can rise. There are three ways to build gluten: kneading, time, and stretch and fold. The more time the dough has, the less kneading it needs. That is why dough that rests a long time in the fridge is kneaded more lightly.

## Salt, sugar and oil

- **Salt** adds flavour, makes the gluten stronger and slows the yeast down. The New York dough uses {{ recipes.styles.new_york.dough.salt.value }}% of the flour weight.
- **Sugar** feeds the yeast a little and helps the crust brown earlier ({{ recipes.styles.new_york.dough.sugar.value }}% in New York).
- **Oil** makes the dough softer and the crust a little crisper ({{ recipes.styles.new_york.dough.oil.value }}% in New York).

Traditional Neapolitan dough has no oil and no sugar, because a very hot oven browns the crust by itself. In a {{ recipes.oven.max_c.value }} degree home oven they can help, so see the Neapolitan page.

## Yeast, time and temperature

The warmer the dough, the faster the yeast works. So you either use more yeast and little time, or little yeast and a lot of time. A long, cool rise gives more flavour. The fridge slows the yeast down, which is why cold dough can rest for days.

Yeast amounts are ranges, not fixed numbers. They depend on your fridge temperature, your yeast and your flour. Sources for the same schedule differ by up to {{ recipes.yeast.source_spread_x.value }} times. Use the calculator for the amount that fits your style and method, then change it after you bake. Fresh yeast counts as {{ recipes.yeast.fresh_to_dry.value }} times the dry amount.

Some people say a long cold rise is easier to digest. This guide does not make that claim. Choose it for the flavour.

## Reading the dough

The times in this guide are a guide for a room of about {{ recipes.oven.room_c.value }} degrees. Your kitchen is warmer in summer and colder in winter, and that changes the speed a lot. In a warm kitchen the dough can be ready much sooner. In a cold one it needs longer. Watch the dough, not the clock. Reading the dough comes with practice, and each bake teaches you a bit more.

- **Ready:** about {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.rise_factor.range.0 }} to {{ recipes.styles.new_york.practice.rise_factor.range.1 }} times its size. It is soft and airy, with small bubbles under the surface and at the bottom of the container. Press a floured finger into it: the dent fills back slowly, about halfway.
- **Too little rise (it needs more time):** it has barely grown, and it feels tight and dense. It springs back at once when you press or stretch it. Give it more time in a warmer spot.
- **Too much rise:** it is more than double, flat and spreading, with big bubbles and a strong smell of alcohol. It is weak and tears when you stretch it, and the crust stays pale. Bake it soon. A cold dough can go back into the fridge. Next time, give it less time or keep it cooler.

> Tip: Mark the level of the dough on the outside of the container with tape or a pen. Then you can see how much it has grown.

## Fermentation and its names

Fermentation is the yeast eating sugar and making gas and flavour. Cold fermentation means letting the dough do this in the fridge. Other names for the same thing are cold proofing and cold retarding. Dough that rises at room temperature has a room-temperature rise; in a warm place, a warm rise.

## Raw dough

Do not eat or taste raw dough. Raw flour can carry harmful bacteria, such as E. coli, that only baking kills.

## Where the styles come from

These are the common stories. Sources often disagree, and none of this changes the recipes.

- **New York:** it is traditionally said to come from Italian immigrants in the early 20th century. People disagree about which pizza restaurant in the United States was the first.
- **Neapolitan:** it comes from Naples. A popular story says the Margherita was made for a queen in the 19th century, but people dispute it. UNESCO listed the art of the Neapolitan pizzaiuolo (the pizza maker), not the pizza itself.
