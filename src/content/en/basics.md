## Hydration

Hydration is the weight of water as a percentage of the weight of flour. The New York dough in this guide has {{ recipes.styles.new_york.dough.hydration.value }}% hydration: that many grams of water for every 100 g of flour.

- Stiffer dough (for example Roman tonda, {{ recipes.styles.roman_tonda.dough.hydration.value }}%) is easy to handle and bakes crisp.
- Softer, stickier dough (for example al taglio, {{ recipes.styles.al_taglio.dough.hydration.value }}%) gives an open, airy crumb and suits a pan.

Why a home oven keeps hydration moderate: an oven that tops out at {{ recipes.oven.max_c.value }} degrees gives less heat, so the bake is longer. With a lot of water the dough can dry out before it browns.

## Gluten

Gluten is a stretchy network that forms when the proteins in flour meet water. It holds the gas the yeast makes, so the dough can rise. There are three ways to build it: kneading, time, and stretch and fold. The more time the dough has, the less kneading it needs, which is why cold-fermented dough is kneaded more lightly.

## Salt, sugar and oil

- **Salt** adds flavour, strengthens the gluten and keeps the yeast in check. The New York dough uses {{ recipes.styles.new_york.dough.salt.value }}% of the flour weight.
- **Sugar** feeds the yeast a little and helps the crust brown earlier ({{ recipes.styles.new_york.dough.sugar.value }}% in New York).
- **Oil** makes the dough softer and the crust a little crisper ({{ recipes.styles.new_york.dough.oil.value }}% in New York).

Traditional Neapolitan dough has no oil and no sugar, because a very hot oven browns the crust by itself. In a {{ recipes.oven.max_c.value }} degree home oven they can help; see the Neapolitan page.

## Yeast, time and temperature

The warmer the dough, the faster the yeast works. So you either use more yeast and little time, or little yeast and a lot of time. A long, cool rise gives more flavour. The fridge slows the yeast down, which is why a cold dough can rest for days.

Yeast amounts are ranges, not fixed numbers. They depend on your fridge temperature, your yeast and your flour, and sources for the same schedule differ by up to {{ recipes.yeast.source_spread_x.value }} times. Use the calculator for the amount that fits your style and method, then adjust after you bake. Fresh yeast counts as {{ recipes.yeast.fresh_to_dry.value }} times the dry amount.

Some people find a long cold rise easier to digest. This guide does not claim it as a fact; it is a reason to choose it for flavour.

## Fermentation and its names

Fermentation is the yeast eating sugar and making gas and flavour. Cold fermentation means letting the dough do this in the fridge. Other names for the same thing are cold proofing and cold retarding. The faster version at room temperature or in a warm place is called a warm or straight rise.

## Where the styles come from

Dates and stories here are the common accounts. Sources often disagree, and none of this affects the recipes.

- **New York:** Italian immigrants, early 20th century. Lombardi's (1905) is traditionally said to be the first pizzeria in the United States, but that claim is disputed.
- **Neapolitan:** Naples. The story that the Margherita was made for the queen in 1889 is popular and disputed. UNESCO listed the art of the Neapolitan pizzaiuolo (the pizza maker), not the pizza itself.
- **Roman tonda and al taglio:** Rome. The modern forms date from the middle of the 20th century; sources give different years.
- **Detroit:** Buddy's Rendezvous is usually credited with the first square pie in 1946. Who created the recipe, and where the first pans came from, is disputed.
- **Chicago deep dish:** Pizzeria Uno, 1943, is the date most often given. Who invented deep dish is disputed.
